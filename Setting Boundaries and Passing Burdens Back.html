Learning to Pass the Burden Back

Protecting Yourself in a Broken System

The Situation

You are a teacher and mother managing significant anxiety—including frequent heart palpitations, persistent stress, and exhaustion that doesn't ease even with sleep. You feel unsupported at work, blamed for problems outside your control, and stretched thin by a lack of basic resources. Financial pressure has recently added another layer of strain.

At home, guilt about parenting and disrupted nights with your son compound the fatigue. Across both settings, you are carrying more than your share, with little acknowledgment or support from those who have the power to help.

Core Problem

The central issue is a long-standing pattern of absorbing blame and responsibility for problems that are not yours. At work, this means compensating for systemic failures—buying your own supplies, working on inadequate equipment at home—rather than directing that accountability upward. The result is chronic anxiety, diminished self-worth, and a sense of being trapped.

Notably, how you see yourself ("mean," "not a good mom") does not match how others experience you. Students and your son respond to you positively. The distorted self-view keeps you from protecting your own needs and reinforces the cycle of overextension.

Understanding the Dynamics

AnxietyPhysical and emotional symptoms—palpitations, worry, dread—that are a rational response to impossible demands, not a personal failing.
AccountabilityOwning your own actions only; not accepting responsibility for outcomes driven by others' failures or lack of resources.
BoundariesDefined limits that protect your time, energy, and well-being by making clear what you can and cannot do without adequate support.
ModelingYour son learns how to handle stress and authority by watching you; how you protect yourself teaches him whether he is allowed to protect himself.

Framework: The Burden-Passing Method

Steps

  • 1Identify your actual core responsibilities and job description.
  • 2Recognize when you are being asked to do more without the resources to do it.
  • 3Communicate the gap in writing to those who are responsible for supplying what you need.
  • 4Document every request you make and every response—or non-response—you receive.
  • 5When blamed for outcomes tied to missing resources, refer to your documentation calmly.
  • 6Do not compensate for systemic failures with your own money, time, or energy.
How This Applies to You Instead of buying scissors or working on a slow personal laptop, you send administration a factual note: "I am unable to complete X because I do not have Y." You log it. If criticism follows, your documentation shows you acted responsibly. The burden stays with the people who hold the resources—not with you.

Practical Instructions

  • When a resource is missing, notify administration in writing before attempting to improvise a solution yourself.
  • Use factual, neutral language: state the need, state the impact, stop there—no emotional framing.
  • Keep a running log of every request, email sent, and response received.
  • If you receive criticism for a gap you documented, point back to your record—calmly, without defensiveness.

Assignment

Before the next session, check your email (including spam) for session notes. Start a simple log of any unmet resource requests at work. Draft and send one professional email to administration about a specific missing resource or support. After sending it, write a few sentences about how it felt—before and after. Bring your log and any responses to the next appointment.

Key Takeaways

Your anxiety is a rational response to an unsupportive system, not a character flaw.
You are not responsible for outcomes caused by others' failures to provide resources.
Others perceive you as competent and caring—trust that feedback over your inner critic.
Documentation is your professional protection; start it now, not after a conflict.
Being criticized or yelled at does not mean you were wrong.
Protecting yourself models for your son that self-protection is acceptable and necessary.

Bottom Line

Your exhaustion is not evidence of failure—it is the predictable result of carrying weight that belongs to others. Clarifying what is genuinely yours to own, communicating needs in writing, and letting administration hold their own accountability are the concrete steps that reduce anxiety and reclaim your sense of agency.

This work extends beyond the workplace. Every time you set a clear limit and refuse unearned blame, you demonstrate to your son that people who are competent and caring still deserve protection. That is not a small thing.
