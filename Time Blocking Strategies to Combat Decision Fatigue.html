Optimizing Energy & Reducing Fatigue

Time Blocking for Sustainable Productivity

The Situation

Your professional life involves a wide range of responsibilities — student meetings, guest lecturing, intern supervision, a sleep hygiene clinic, outreach events, research, adjunct teaching, and a steady stream of administrative work. Most tasks are flexible in timing, which provides freedom but also creates an unstructured workday prone to frequent interruptions, including unscheduled drop-ins from a mentee.

With no consistent routine anchoring the week, the sheer variety of tasks forces constant on-the-spot decisions about what to do next. The result is a sense of being chronically drained despite a workload that is manageable in theory.

Core Problem

The primary driver of fatigue here is not the volume of work — it is the pattern of constant task switching and unplanned decision-making. When each hour requires you to choose what to do next and then mentally shift gears, cognitive resources deplete quickly. An open schedule, without intentional structure, amplifies this drain rather than relieving it.

Understanding the Dynamics

Decision FatigueMental exhaustion from making too many choices throughout the day, leading to reduced focus and poorer decisions as the day progresses.
Task SwitchingThe cognitive cost of jumping between unrelated tasks — each switch disrupts focus and requires extra mental effort to re-orient.
Time BlockingA scheduling strategy that groups similar tasks into dedicated time windows, reducing the need to decide in the moment and protecting focused work.
Energy OptimizationArranging your workday to match task demands with your available mental energy, and eliminating unnecessary drains on that energy.

Framework: Time Blocking for Energy Optimization

  1. 1List every recurring weekly responsibility — meetings, teaching, admin, research, outreach.
  2. 2Group tasks by the type of thinking they require (e.g., relational, administrative, creative).
  3. 3Assign each group to a consistent, dedicated time block in your weekly calendar.
  4. 4During each block, work only on that category — no switching to unrelated tasks.
  5. 5Build short buffer periods between blocks to reset before shifting focus.
  6. 6At week's end, briefly review what worked and adjust blocks as needed.
How This Applies to You For example: schedule all student addiction meetings in one afternoon block, batch emails into two set windows daily, and protect specific mornings for research or teaching prep. Rather than reacting to tasks as they arise, you decide in advance — eliminating dozens of small in-the-moment choices each week.

Practical Instructions

  • At the start of each week, map out your time blocks before the week begins.
  • Batch emails and admin into two or three set windows — not throughout the day.
  • Schedule your mentee's check-ins at a set time to eliminate unplanned drop-ins.
  • Do not allow urgent-but-unimportant tasks to break into a focused block.

Assignment

Before the next session, draft a full weekly schedule using time blocks for each major work category. List your recurring tasks, group them by type, assign each group to a specific block, and follow the draft for one week. Note how your energy levels feel across the week — what felt easier, what still felt disruptive, and where the structure held or broke down.

Key Takeaways

Decision fatigue — not workload — is the primary energy drain.
Task switching carries a real cognitive cost each time it happens.
Batching similar tasks reduces how often you shift mental gears.
A consistent schedule eliminates many small daily decisions.
Buffer time between blocks prevents cumulative burnout.
Schedule flexibility is a strength — structure makes it work for you.

Bottom Line

Today's session established that the path to sustainable energy is not doing less — it is organizing what you already do into a more deliberate structure. Grouping similar tasks and protecting focused blocks directly reduces the decision-making load that is draining you.

Your immediate next step is to draft and test a structured weekly schedule. Bring your observations to the next session — what shifted in your energy, what disrupted the blocks, and what adjustments make sense. This is an iterative process, not a one-time fix.
