Alignment Psychiatry

Navigating Expectations & Building Consideration in Friendships

Therapeutic Insights Document — Individual Session

The Situation

Social engagement has been increasing — concerts, festivals, plans with friends — yet the unpredictability of others is leaving you physically and emotionally drained. When plans shift or friends are unreliable, disappointment follows quickly and lingers.

To avoid being let down, you tend to suppress your own preferences and accommodate others even when inconvenienced. Past experiences of being deprioritized have created a habit of anticipating worst-case outcomes, even with friends who have not earned that expectation.

Core Problem

The central tension is between wanting genuine connection and fearing the disappointment that comes with unmet expectations. Suppressing needs to avoid conflict results in feeling undervalued — and the cycle repeats.

The key distortion: your feelings often reflect old experiences rather than current evidence. Not every unreturned call or changed plan signals rejection — many lapses are accidental, not malicious.

Understanding the Dynamics

Automatic Thoughts

Reflexive interpretations that surface instantly, shaped by past hurt. They often misread neutral situations as threatening.

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT)

A method for identifying unhelpful thoughts and replacing them with ones that fit the actual, present situation.

Intentional vs. Unintentional Behavior

A single lapse is likely an accident; a repeated pattern is information. The difference determines the appropriate response.

Consideration

Being genuinely factored into another person's plans and decisions — a measurable quality revealed when you express a need and observe how they respond.

Frameworks

1 — Challenging Automatic Thoughts (CBT)

  • 1Notice the automatic thought (e.g., "She doesn't want to hang out with me anymore").
  • 2Identify its source — is it rooted in past experience or present evidence?
  • 3Evaluate whether the thought actually fits the current situation.
  • 4Communicate your feelings and needs directly to the person involved.
  • 5Observe their response and update your belief based on what happens.
Applied here: When a friend missed a call before a planned meetup, the immediate thought was rejection — but this friend had never done that before. Pausing to check the evidence, then speaking up, led to an apology and improved communication next time.

2 — Distinguishing Accidents from Patterns

  • 1Assess whether the behavior appears to be a one-time accident or a recurring pattern.
  • 2Extend genuine forgiveness for isolated, unintentional lapses.
  • 3Name the pattern when it repeats — don't quietly absorb it.
  • 4Observe whether the person acknowledges it and adjusts their behavior.
  • 5Set a clear boundary if the carelessness continues.
Applied here: Repeatedly excusing careless behavior signals to others that it is acceptable. Communicating disappointment is not punitive — it provides context and tests whether you are genuinely considered.

Practical Instructions

  • When a strong reaction hits, pause and ask: "Is this based on this person's actual behavior, or on older experiences?"
  • If a friend's behavior inconveniences you, say so — describe how it affected you rather than brushing it aside.
  • Watch the response: does the person acknowledge it and adjust, or does the behavior repeat?
  • Forgive genuine accidents once; treat a repeated pattern as information and respond with a boundary.

Assignment

Between now and the next session, practice catching automatic thoughts in real time — especially when plans change, a friend is late, or someone is unresponsive. Write down the thought, ask whether it reflects this person's actual history or someone else's, decide whether to communicate your feelings, and note what happens when you do. Bring two or three concrete examples to the next session.

Key Takeaways

Past disappointments shape present expectations — but they are not the same thing.

Automatic thoughts often reflect old patterns, not current reality.

Expressing a need is not "making a fuss" — it is how you learn whether you are valued.

A considerate friend will acknowledge impact and adjust; watch for this signal.

One lapse is an accident; repeated lapses are a pattern that warrants a response.

Staying silent about patterns teaches others that the behavior is acceptable.

Bottom Line

The core work right now is learning to separate what has happened before from what is actually happening now. Automatic thoughts move fast and feel certain — but they can be checked. When you pause, evaluate the evidence, and speak up, you give the relationship a chance to show you what it actually is.

Communicating disappointment is not a risk to the friendship — it is a reasonable test of it. Friends who consider you will respond to honest expression. That is the goal: not fewer expectations, but clearer ones, expressed directly enough to be met.
