Harnessing Strengths & Building Intentional Focus

Navigating New Opportunities with Purpose and Clarity

The Situation

You are transitioning between jobs, with a new paid church position that may allow you to leave a less satisfying library role. Current medication is supporting improved energy and focus throughout the workday, which is a meaningful shift.

At the same time, challenges with attention, memory, and follow-through remain — particularly in managing multiple responsibilities and communicating effectively in important relationships. Clarifying work-life boundaries and advocating for personal needs, including exploring FMLA, are live concerns.

Core Problem

The central difficulty is translating vision and capability into consistent action — especially in unfamiliar roles or under pressure. There is a recurring pattern of internalizing expectations and underadvocating for your own strengths, which limits opportunities for recognition and growth.

Missed details in conversation and scattered task management compound this, making it harder to follow through even when motivation is present.

Understanding the Dynamics

VisualizationMentally rehearsing a desired outcome — how it looks and feels — to guide planning. It bridges abstract goals and concrete steps.
IntentionalityActing with deliberate purpose rather than on autopilot. Applied to both planning new projects and communicating in key relationships.
Empathy as ResearchDrawing on others' documented experiences to fill gaps in your own. Useful when tackling unfamiliar tasks where direct experience is limited.
Cognitive OverloadWhen too many systems or demands compete for attention, follow-through breaks down. Consolidating inputs reduces this strain significantly.

Frameworks

1 — Visualization & Iterative Planning

  1. 1Visualize the desired outcome — focus on how it should feel, not just what it looks like.
  2. 2Identify what you already know and what you still need to learn.
  3. 3Research examples or seek input to fill gaps in experience.
  4. 4Write down ideas and plans to fully process them outside your head.
  5. 5Revisit and refine — allow multiple passes before committing to a final plan.
Applied here: Use this when preparing for new church responsibilities (e.g., AV setup, project leadership). Imagining the end result first helps you work backward to realistic steps and communicate your plan to others.

2 — Intentional Communication

  1. 1During conversations, take a brief note or repeat back what was just said.
  2. 2Explain your intent — let the other person know why you're doing this.
  3. 3Name your worry out loud before it becomes a barrier ("I'm concerned this might seem odd, but…").
Applied here: In close relationships, explaining why you're taking notes turns a potential misread into a sign of care. Naming the worry preempts the misunderstanding entirely.

Practical Instructions

  • Dedicate time to visualize new projects — write what you know, what you need, and how success should feel.
  • Use one and only one to-do list (paper or digital); check and update it every day.
  • In key conversations, take notes or repeat back main points and briefly explain why.
  • When anxious about how something will land, say the worry aloud to the other person first.

Assignment

Before the next session: contact HR to ask about FMLA eligibility and time-off options — call or email, take notes on what you learn, and bring that information in. Also begin using a single to-do list daily and practice the intentional listening approach in at least one important conversation this week.

Key Takeaways

Visualize outcomes by feel, then work backward to steps.
Iterative planning is normal — first drafts are supposed to be incomplete.
One consolidated to-do list reduces forgetfulness and cognitive load.
Explaining your listening strategies builds trust rather than confusion.
Naming a worry out loud is itself a communication tool.
Advocating for your needs — including FMLA — is a clinical priority, not an extra.

Bottom Line

The core theme of this session was intentionality — in how you plan for new responsibilities and how you show up in important conversations. The frameworks here are practical, not abstract: they are designed for someone managing real attention challenges while navigating a meaningful professional transition.

Medication is providing a stronger foundation. The next layer is structural: one list, deliberate communication, and advocating clearly for the time and support you need. These are not minor adjustments — they directly shape whether your strengths become visible to others and to yourself.
