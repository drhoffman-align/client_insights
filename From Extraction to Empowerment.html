🔋 From Extraction to Empowerment: Reclaiming Self-Care and Agency in Service-Oriented Work

Giving 77% of your energy to others and 23% to yourself is a measurable imbalance, not just a feeling

The Situation

A demanding schedule as a health science administrator at a federal agency — four 10-hour days, 8am to 6pm — runs alongside preparation for FMLA leave starting September 21st, taken for chronic health conditions requiring ongoing treatment. Identity and self-worth are deeply tied to productivity and service to others, both at work and at home.

A persistent sense of being extracted by the environment shows up clearly — the majority of energy goes to others, with little left over. Negative self-talk, difficulty prioritizing personal needs, and a sense of being trapped by responsibility round out the picture.

Core Problem

A pattern of self-neglect in favor of serving others has produced exhaustion, diminished well-being, and a real lack of motivation. Value gets equated with productivity, and attempts to prioritize personal needs bring guilt or self-criticism instead of relief.

There's a mismatch between the felt belief that service must be constant to justify existence, and the reality that sustainable service actually requires self-care and boundaries. The result has been chronic depletion and an absence of intentional self-care.

"If you aren't okay, your service isn't okay."

Understanding the Dynamics

Identity-Productivity Fusion

Self-worth tied directly to output and service — a fusion that demands more and more productivity just to feel secure.

Learned Servitude

An early-taught pattern of constant service to others, absorbed so thoroughly it can feel like personality rather than something learned.

Negative Self-Talk as Self-Sabotage

Calling your own choices "stupid" undermines the exact self-trust needed to make sustainable ones going forward.

Well-Being as Resistance

Protecting your own well-being functions as resistance against systems built to extract without reciprocating — not just personal comfort.

Framework A

Survival, Service, and Extraction

1
Recognize the basic human drive to survive and form relationships for mutual support.
2
Identify when service to others becomes extraction — giving more than you receive.
3
Assess the feedback loop — are your efforts reciprocated, or are you being drained?
4
Acknowledge your decision-making capacity — you can choose where to invest your energy.
Applied Roughly 77% of waking hours go to others, leaving 23% for anything else — the result of being "taught to be an indentured servant" without ever learning another way to be. That's the shape of extraction made concrete.

Framework B

Motivation vs. Discipline

1
Understand the difference — motivation is driven by positive emotion; discipline is acting despite its absence.
2
Recognize that discipline requires more energy and is less sustainable long-term.
3
Identify sources of energy — emotion, willpower, physical needs.
4
Shift toward motivation by aligning actions with personal meaning and self-benefit.
Applied Almost everything currently runs on discipline — pushing through negative self-talk rather than genuine motivation. Finding ways for work and life to feed energy back, not just drain it, is the actual target.

Discipline

  • Powered by willpower alone
  • Draining, harder to sustain
  • Works despite how you feel

Motivation

  • Powered by positive emotion
  • Energizing, easier to sustain
  • Works because it means something

Framework C

Self-Centered Decision-Making

1
Inventory how much time and energy is spent on self versus others.
2
Reframe self-care as essential, not selfish.
3
Practice intentional self-care by planning and prioritizing personal needs.
Applied Noticing just how little energy currently goes to personal needs is the starting point. Being "you-centered" isn't framed as indulgence here — it's what makes sustainable service to anyone else actually possible.
"Your well-being is fighting systems. Your well-being is fighting for humanity."

Practical Instructions

  • Before taking on a new task at work, ask what you'll get back from it — recognition, growth, rest, anything — before saying yes.
  • Pick one small task each day and do it purely because you want to, not because anyone is expecting it.
  • When you notice yourself pushing through on willpower alone, name it as "discipline mode" and ask what would make it "motivation mode" instead.
  • Keep a short list of the roles that consistently take more than they give back, separate from the ones that feel mutual.
  • When FMLA leave begins, protect at least one hour a day that has no productive purpose attached to it at all.

Assignment

During your FMLA transition, spend a few hours each day listing the roles you carry — work, home, relationships — noting what you do, who benefits, and what you get back. Identify which activities are for you and which are for others, and reflect on how your energy might rebalance. Bring your lists and reflections to the next session.

Key Takeaways

Giving 77% of your energy to others and 23% to yourself is a measurable imbalance, not just a feeling.
Operating on discipline alone is more exhausting than operating on motivation, even when the output looks the same.
A pattern of over-service that was taught early doesn't have to be treated as a fixed personality trait.
Self-care functions as a form of resistance against systems that extract without reciprocating, not just a personal indulgence.
Calling your own decisions "stupid" undermines the exact self-trust needed to make better ones.
A relationship or role that only takes, without any return, is extraction — regardless of how necessary it feels.
Choosing where to invest energy is a real form of agency, even inside a demanding, inflexible system.
Protecting your well-being isn't separate from serving others well — it's the actual foundation for being able to.

Bottom Line

Spending the overwhelming majority of available energy on others, while running mostly on willpower, isn't dedication in the healthy sense. It's an extraction pattern that was taught early and never examined — and examining it now is what makes a different way possible.

Value that's tied entirely to output will always demand more output to feel secure, which is exactly the trap that's produced this level of depletion. The fix isn't working harder. It's decoupling worth from productivity in the first place.

This period isn't just about physical treatment. It's the first real chance in a long time to find out what 23% versus 77% could look like reversed, even briefly, before deciding what to carry back into work afterward.
