Alignment Psychiatry

From Extraction to Empowerment

Reclaiming Self-Care and Agency in Service-Oriented Work

The Situation

This client works as a health science administrator at a federal agency, managing a demanding four-day, 10-hour-per-day schedule. She is preparing to begin FMLA leave due to chronic health conditions that require ongoing treatment and attention.

Her sense of worth is closely tied to productivity and service to others. She reports feeling drained by her environment — giving the majority of her energy to work and relationships while leaving very little for herself. This pattern is accompanied by negative self-talk and difficulty recognizing her own needs as legitimate.

Core Problem

The central issue is a longstanding pattern of self-neglect driven by the belief that her value depends on what she does for others. She feels guilty when she attempts to prioritize herself and pushes forward using sheer discipline rather than genuine motivation — a approach that is costly and unsustainable.

The result is chronic depletion: she is giving from an empty reserve, which ultimately undermines both her well-being and the quality of her service to others.

Understanding the Dynamics

ExtractionOccurs when you consistently give more than you receive, leaving you depleted without replenishment.
Feedback LoopThe reciprocal exchange of energy in relationships or work — a broken loop accelerates extraction.
Motivation vs. DisciplineMotivation is fueled by meaning and positive emotion; discipline is acting despite its absence — and costs far more energy.
Decision-Making CapacityThe inherent ability to choose where and how to invest your energy, even within demanding systems.

Frameworks

1 — Survival, Service, and Extraction Model

1
Recognize the basic human drive to form relationships for mutual support.
2
Identify when service to others has crossed into extraction — giving without receiving.
3
Assess your feedback loops: are your efforts reciprocated or are you being drained?
4
Acknowledge your agency in deciding where to invest your energy going forward.
How this applies: Roughly 77% of waking hours currently go to others, leaving only 23% for personal needs. Recognizing this imbalance is the starting point for reclaiming agency and setting protective boundaries.

2 — Shifting from Discipline to Motivation

1
Distinguish discipline (pushing through) from motivation (drawn by meaning or positive emotion).
2
Recognize that sustained discipline without motivation accelerates burnout and depletion.
3
Identify what sources of energy remain available — emotional, physical, relational.
4
Actively align actions with personal meaning so that effort feels sustaining, not draining.
How this applies: Negative self-talk has been the primary engine driving action. The goal is to replace that with intentional choices connected to genuine self-interest and personal value — not obligation.

Practical Instructions

  • Pause before acting — sit with what's needed before moving into task mode.
  • Schedule at least one daily block of time that is solely for you, even if brief.
  • List your roles and cross-match each with available resources: time, support, and skills.
  • When negative self-talk surfaces, name it and reframe it as information — not judgment.

Assignment

Each day during the FMLA transition period, set aside 2–3 hours to reflect on your roles, responsibilities, and energy. List what you do, who benefits, and what returns to you. Identify which activities serve you and which serve others exclusively. Cross-match your resources to each role and note where rebalancing is possible. Bring your lists and observations to the next session.

Key Takeaways

Your worth is not defined by how much you do for others.
Extraction is real — notice where you give without receiving.
You have the right and capacity to protect your own energy.
Sustainable service requires sustainable self-care.
Motivation is more efficient and less costly than discipline alone.
Rebalancing toward yourself is not selfish — it is necessary.

Bottom Line

This session surfaced a deeply ingrained pattern: self-worth tied to output, energy flowing outward without replenishment, and discipline substituting for genuine motivation. The frameworks introduced are tools for interrupting that cycle — not by withdrawing from service, but by ensuring that service is no longer funded by depletion.

The work ahead begins with awareness: tracking where energy goes, identifying what returns, and making small, deliberate choices to direct more toward personal well-being. Self-care in this context is not comfort — it is a structural requirement for functioning and for continuing to show up for others.
