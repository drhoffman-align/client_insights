🌊 Longing, Loss, and Connection

Navigating Grief and Yearning for a Deeper Bond

The Situation

You are holding a specific kind of grief — not just the fact of your father's death, but the ongoing accumulation of moments that will not happen. The milestones you imagined him present for. The conversations you planned to have. The version of yourself you wanted to show him — the artist, the businesswoman, the person you have become — and the particular ache of knowing he didn't get to see it.

Around you, others who loved him seem to be settling into a different relationship with the loss — calmer, more forward-moving. And that contrast amplifies your own yearning. It's not that you're grieving wrong. It's that you're grieving something specific, and that something is still very much unfinished.

Core Problem

The central issue is a gap between what you deeply want — to know your father more fully, to be known by him, to have him present for the life still unfolding — and what is now possible. This is not a problem that can be solved. But it is one that can be moved through, in ways that are meaningful and that allow the relationship to continue in a different form.

There is also the particular difficulty of watching someone else's grief move at a different pace than yours. Grief is not a race, and there is no position of "ahead" or "behind." Your longing is not a sign that you're stuck. It is a sign that the connection mattered — and that you are not yet done with it.

"The longing is what I always feel — I'm always reaching out as far as I can, and I feel like I'll never be able to touch him."

Understanding the Dynamics

Grief as Yearning

Grief is not only sadness. It is also longing, desire for contact, frustration, and love that has nowhere to go. The yearning you feel — reaching for connection that can't happen in the way you imagined — is grief in its most specific form.

Unattainable vs. Unreachable

Some wishes cannot happen in their literal form — your father cannot walk you down the aisle. But the underlying need — to feel accompanied, witnessed, held by his presence in that moment — may have other expressions. Grief work is finding those expressions.

Meaning-Making

The process of finding ways to carry a relationship forward after loss — through memory, story, ritual, and new forms of connection. It doesn't erase the grief. It gives the love somewhere to go beyond the loss itself.

Non-Linear Grief

People grieve at different paces, in different ways, and with different timelines. Someone appearing more settled is not evidence that your grief is wrong or that you are behind. It may simply mean they are working through different aspects of the same loss.

🌿 Framework: Grief Processing and Meaning-Making

A five-step process for moving from stuck longing to active connection

This framework is not about "getting over" your father. It is about finding forms of connection that are still available — and about moving grief from something that happens to you toward something you can actively work with.

1

Identify and Name the Specific Longing

Not just "I miss my father" — but the specific things. What conversation did you want to have? What version of yourself did you want to show him? What moment did you want him present for? The more specific the longing, the more clearly you can look for what might honor it.

2

Examine Expectations — Gently

Which of your wishes can no longer happen in their literal form? Naming them — not to dismiss them, but to be accurate about them — frees you to look for alternative expressions. You can hold the grief for what cannot happen and still look for what can.

3

Seek Him Out in New Ways

Most people don't think about this. Your father was someone before he was your father. People knew him — his humor, his quirks, the way he handled things, what he cared about. Those people carry parts of him. Asking them to share is not grief management. It's relationship.

4

Gather the Stories

Family members, friends, colleagues, people from different seasons of his life — each of them holds a different lens on who he was. The fuller the picture you build, the more of him you have access to. You are not being morbid. You are expanding the relationship.

5

Choose What You Want to Believe

You get to decide what you believe about his ongoing presence. Whether that's spiritual, symbolic, or expressed through the ways you carry him — in your values, your work, the way you move through the world. No one else gets to set that belief for you.

Putting It Into Practice

  • Make the list — who knew him, and how. Not just family. Friends from different periods of his life. People he worked with. People who saw sides of him you didn't. The list itself is a form of seeking.

  • Ask open-ended questions when you reach out. "What is your favorite memory of him?" "What was he like as a friend?" "What would he have made of [something in your life now]?" Let people talk. You're not looking for facts — you're looking for texture.

  • Bring him to the milestones he won't physically attend. Decide how you want to acknowledge his absence — and his presence — at the moments that matter. This is your choice. No one else gets to decide what form that takes.

  • Let your creative work be a form of conversation. The art you make now exists in the world he once existed in. That is not nothing. What you create in this period carries the loss and the love in a form that outlasts both.

  • Stop measuring your grief against others'. Someone appearing more settled is not evidence they're further along or that you're behind. They are working through different material. Let your longing be what it is without requiring it to look like anyone else's.

Assignment

Reach Out to Two People Who Knew Him

Identify at least two people — outside your immediate family — who knew your father in a context you didn't see directly. Reach out and ask them to share a memory or story. It doesn't have to be a long conversation. "I've been thinking about my dad and I'd love to hear a memory of him if you're willing to share" is enough to start.

Write down what you learn. Bring your notes and any reflections — including how it felt to seek him out this way — to the next session.

Key Takeaways

Grief includes yearning — the persistent desire for connection that can no longer happen in its literal form. That yearning is not a malfunction. It is evidence of the bond.
Some wishes cannot happen literally. The underlying need — to be witnessed, to know him better, to feel accompanied — can find other expressions. Naming the literal loss opens the door to finding those alternatives.
Your father existed before he was your father. People carry different versions of him. Seeking out those versions is not grief management — it is an expansion of the relationship into forms that are still available.
Comparing your grief pace to someone else's amplifies the feeling of being behind or wrong. Grief moves differently for everyone — because each relationship is different, and each loss hits different angles.
The family lineage research and the outreach you've already begun are active forms of seeking. You're already doing this work. The assignment builds on what you've started.
You get to decide what you believe about his ongoing presence in your life. That belief is yours to choose — spiritual, symbolic, or held in the way you carry him forward. No one else sets those terms.
Creative work — the art, the writing, the building — is one of the forms the relationship can take. What you make in this period is made in the same world he once occupied. That continuity is real.
Processing grief is not letting go of love or longing. It is finding new forms for it — ones that allow it to move through you rather than staying stuck in the shape of what can no longer be.

🌊 The Bottom Line

Your longing for your father is not a problem to solve. It is love in the form available after loss — and it deserves to be taken seriously rather than hurried past. What this session opened is the possibility of a different kind of seeking: not waiting to feel closer to him, but actively going to find him in the forms he still exists in.

He is in the stories other people carry about him. He is in the lineage you're tracing. He is in the work you make and the person you've become. None of that is the same as having him present. All of it is real. Grief does not require you to choose between missing what's gone and honoring what remains — it asks you to hold both, for as long as both are true.

The reaching you described — arms and legs extended as far as they can go — does not have to stay that way. Not because you stop reaching, but because you find more things to reach toward that actually respond. That is what this work is for.
