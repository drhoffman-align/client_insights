Taking Ownership

Reclaiming Agency and Value in Work and Life

The Situation

You are managing overlapping demands as a full-time teacher, part-time therapist, and parent to a young child. The emotional weight of working with children throughout the day — and again in the evenings — leaves little energy for yourself or your son. Financial pressure compounds this, with daycare costs and limited family support stretching your resources thin.

Professionally, you feel undervalued and uncertain about how to push back on compensation or workload. A difficult student and unsupportive parents at work add friction, making an already taxing situation feel harder to navigate.

Core Problem

The central difficulty is allowing others — employers, supervisors, family — to determine your worth and direction rather than asserting your own. This pattern of deference creates chronic dissatisfaction, a sense of being stuck, and ongoing burnout.

There is a clear gap between wanting more control over your life and hesitating to take the steps that would create it. Notably, you advocate readily for others but consistently stop short of doing the same for yourself.

Understanding the Dynamics

AgencyThe capacity to act on your own terms. Right now, agency is limited by deferring to others' judgments about your role and worth.
Self-AdvocacyActively representing your own interests. This is especially difficult when it involves compensation, workload, or pushing back on authority.
BoundariesLimits that protect your time and energy. Without them, you absorb tasks and expectations that extend well beyond your core role.
BurnoutExhaustion from sustained emotional labor without adequate recovery. Working with children all day and in therapy evenings leaves no buffer.

Framework: Reclaiming Agency Through Self-Advocacy

  1. 1
    Recognize passivity — Notice where you let others set the terms for your work and worth.
  2. 2
    Clarify your needs — Get specific about what you want: pay, schedule, time with your son.
  3. 3
    Start with low-stakes advocacy — Ask a small question, clarify a contract term, decline one extra task.
  4. 4
    Own your value — Identify your skills and contributions and communicate them deliberately.
  5. 5
    Choose intentionally — Weigh each opportunity against your actual needs, not just what's offered.
How This Applies

You have not yet negotiated your billing percentage, though you are eligible. You have also not explored tutoring as a better-fitting income source. Each step above has a direct application point in your current situation.

Practical Instructions

  • Review your employment contract and confirm your current compensation structure.
  • Schedule a meeting with your supervisor specifically to discuss your billing percentage.
  • Track weekly hours and flag time spent on unpaid or out-of-scope tasks.
  • Practice declining at least one extra task this week that falls outside your defined role.

Assignment

Before the next session: locate and review your contract, write a list of your professional contributions and achievements, and initiate a compensation conversation with your supervisor. Identify one boundary you can set this week and follow through on it. Come prepared to report what happened and what felt difficult.

Key Takeaways

Allowing others to define your worth is a pattern, not a fixed trait — it can change.
Burnout is a signal that your current structure is unsustainable, not a personal failing.
You advocate effectively for others — the same skill applies to yourself.
Small acts of self-advocacy build confidence more reliably than large ones.
Reviewing your contract is not confrontational — it is basic professional due diligence.
Accepting your own value is distinct from proving it; the evidence is already there.

Bottom Line

Much of what you have built professionally has come through capability and cooperation — but that path has also left your own needs consistently last. The work ahead involves shifting from responding to what others offer toward deciding what you require and asking for it directly.

The practical steps — contract review, supervisor conversation, boundary-setting — are where this change becomes real. They are not dramatic moves; they are small, deliberate choices that, repeated over time, produce a different kind of life.
