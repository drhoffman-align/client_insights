Finding Your Impact: Control, Feedback, and Clarity

Therapeutic Insights Document — Individual Session

The Situation

You are navigating challenges in your role as a trainer and mentor at work, where you feel limited control over certain dynamics. A desire to be liked can pull you toward avoiding feedback, making it harder to fulfill the mentoring role effectively.

In your personal life, communication with family members often feels effortful and unrewarding. You are looking for ways to assert your influence, protect your peace, and support growth in yourself and others.

Core Problem

The central issue is a pattern of avoiding difficult conversations — at work and at home — to stay comfortable or maintain likability. This avoidance interrupts the feedback that makes growth possible, leading to repeated frustrations and a sense of powerlessness.

The reality is that you have more influence than you recognize. The gap is between where your control actually ends and where you are stepping back too early.

Understanding the Dynamics

Control vs. ImpactControl means determining an outcome; impact means influencing it. You cannot control others, but your actions and words consistently affect them.
Feedback LoopAn ongoing exchange of information that allows both parties to adjust and grow. When feedback stops, so does learning.
AvoidanceSteering around uncomfortable conversations to reduce short-term discomfort. It relieves tension briefly but blocks resolution and reinforces the original problem.
ClarityMaking your intentions, goals, and expectations explicit. Clarity reduces misalignment and helps others engage more independently and effectively.

Framework 1: The Control-Impact Framework

  1. 1Identify what you can and cannot control — your actions vs. others' responses.
  2. 2Recognize where you have impact even without full control.
  3. 3Focus your energy on your own decisions and communication.
  4. 4Accept that you can influence outcomes, but not guarantee them.
Applied: With your trainee, you cannot guarantee understanding — but you can clarify instructions, invite questions, and adjust your approach. With family, you cannot change how they respond, but you can choose how clearly and calmly you communicate.

Framework 2: The Feedback Loop Model

  1. 1Give and receive feedback consistently, not just at crisis points.
  2. 2Use what you learn to adjust your actions and expectations.
  3. 3When giving feedback, connect tasks to the larger goal — explain the "why."
  4. 4Notice avoidance as the signal to engage, not to withdraw.
Applied: Withholding feedback from your trainee to avoid tension keeps both of you stuck. Delivering it clearly — even imperfectly — restores the loop and allows both parties to adjust. The same applies in personal relationships.

Practical Instructions

  • Before reacting, pause and ask: "What do I actually have control over here?"
  • When you notice an issue, communicate it clearly and kindly — don't wait for the perfect moment.
  • When teaching or delegating, explain the "why," not just the steps.
  • Notice when you're tempted to avoid a conversation, and treat that urge as a cue to engage.

Assignment

Identify one situation at work and one in your personal life where you have been avoiding giving feedback or clarifying your needs. For each: write down what you've been holding back, draft a clear and kind version of what you want to say, deliver it before your next session, and note how you felt before and after. Bring your observations to the next appointment.

Key Takeaways

You control your actions and responses — not others' reactions.
Impact and control are different; your influence is real even when control is limited.
Feedback loops are essential — without them, learning stops for everyone.
Avoidance feels like relief but creates more problems over time.
Explaining the "why" helps others think critically, not just follow steps.
Honest feedback serves your own clarity and peace, not just the other person.

Bottom Line

You have more influence than you have been using. The avoidance pattern — rooted in wanting to keep the peace — is quietly costing you the peace you're trying to protect. Feedback, delivered with care and clarity, is the mechanism that keeps relationships and growth moving.

Your task going forward is to practice engaging where you have previously stepped back. Clarity and honest communication are not risks to relationships — they are what makes relationships functional. Trust that leaning in, even when uncomfortable, produces better outcomes than staying silent.
