Alignment Psychiatry

From Self-Advocacy to Self-Valuation

Harnessing Expertise and Setting Boundaries

The Situation

You are managing a high-stress period involving active legal proceedings, advocacy for yourself and others, family responsibilities, and significant travel. This combination creates sustained emotional and physical fatigue that is difficult to recover from without deliberate protection of your time and energy.

At the same time, you have identified new legal resources that may strengthen your case and are preparing for oral arguments and mediation. Medication refills for bipolar disorder and ADHD were addressed and confirmed, supporting continued stability during this demanding stretch.

Core Problem

The central pattern is a longstanding tendency to place others' needs ahead of your own and to offer your expertise without clear boundaries or compensation. Over time, this has produced emotional exhaustion and resentment when your contributions go unrecognized or unrewarded.

There is a gap between how much you expect of yourself and how little others reciprocate. Closing that gap requires actively assigning value to your work and communicating that value before you commit — not after.

Understanding the Dynamics

Self-ValuationAssigning a concrete worth to your skills and time, rather than defaulting to giving them away freely.
Boundary SettingDefining in advance what you will and won't do, and under what conditions — including with friends and family.
Resentment as a SignalFeelings of frustration or depletion indicate that a boundary has been crossed and needs to be reasserted.
ReciprocityThe expectation that effort and support flow in both directions — not just from you to others.

Framework A

Self-Valuation and Boundary Setting

  • 1Recognize and name the specific expertise you are being asked to contribute.
  • 2Estimate what that contribution would cost if you were billing professionally.
  • 3Set clear parameters for your involvement before saying yes.
  • 4Communicate expectations for compensation or reciprocity at the outset.
  • 5Monitor for resentment or exhaustion as signals to revisit the arrangement.
  • 6Protect your own needs and recovery time before extending resources to others.
How This AppliesYou have been providing substantive legal and advocacy support to a friend without formal compensation, framing it as a "test case." Even test cases carry real cost. Formalizing your value — with friends included — is not unkind; it is necessary.

Framework B

Worth vs. Value Model

  • 1Value is internal — the baseline you set for your own work and time.
  • 2Worth is external — what others offer or agree to pay.
  • 3If you don't establish your value, others will set it for you, usually too low.
  • 4Your value is the floor; worth is what gets negotiated above it.
How This AppliesWhen you enter any arrangement — legal, professional, or personal — your value should already be established internally before the conversation begins. Worth can flex; value should not.

Practical Instructions

  • Before agreeing to help, pause and ask yourself: "What do I get from this commitment?"
  • Calculate time and expertise in concrete terms and assign an hourly or flat value.
  • Communicate compensation or reciprocity expectations before starting, not after.
  • Use resentment or fatigue as an active cue to reassess — not something to push through.

Assignment

After each significant commitment or interaction, write a brief journal entry addressing three things: what you contributed, what you received in return, and what motivated you to say yes. Focus specifically on the question "What's in it for me?" Bring any patterns you notice to your next session.

Key Takeaways

Your expertise has real monetary value — treat it that way.
Set boundaries before committing, not after resentment builds.
Value is internal and self-determined; don't let others set your floor.
Resentment and exhaustion are data, not personal failings.
Expecting compensation is professional, not selfish.
Confidence in your worth will not turn you into someone you don't want to be.

Bottom Line

The core shift this session asks of you is straightforward: stop letting your value be assigned by others. You bring substantive skills to every situation you enter, and those skills deserve formal recognition — even in informal relationships. Formalizing that is not a betrayal of generosity; it is what makes generosity sustainable.

As you move into mediation, legal preparation, and ongoing advocacy, the same standard applies: know your value before the conversation starts, protect your energy deliberately, and use journaling to stay accountable to yourself. Medication stability supports all of this — continue as prescribed.
