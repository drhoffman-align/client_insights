Building Trust and Addressing Bias

Supporting Your Child's Adjustment at School

The Situation

About one month ago, your daughter started at a new school. Since then, she has struggled to settle in — withdrawing, using tiredness as an exit, and having tantrums. School staff appear to have formed early negative impressions of her, which shows up in limited positive feedback, exclusion from classroom roles and photos, and less warmth than other children receive.

As a result, you are fielding frequent negative reports from school and carrying significant stress. The picture that comes through is of a child who doesn't feel wanted — and staff who may not yet know how to reach her.

Core Problem

A cycle of mistrust has formed: staff's early negative judgments lead to less engagement and fewer positive interactions, which makes your daughter feel unwelcome, which reinforces the very behaviors staff find difficult. She is being treated as a "problem child" rather than as a child in transition who needs extra support.

Her behaviors — withdrawal, meltdowns, using "I'm sleepy" to opt out — are normal responses to feeling excluded and unseen. The environment is reinforcing the problem rather than resolving it.

Understanding the Dynamics

BiasUnconscious attitudes that lead to differential treatment — visible here in exclusion from photos, fewer roles, and predominantly negative reporting.
Trust-BuildingCreating safety through consistent, warm engagement so a child feels secure enough to participate and connect.
AutonomyOffering choices within limits (e.g., "art or blocks?") gives children a sense of control and reduces resistance and power struggles.
Transition SupportNew environments require extra patience and proactive inclusion — what looks like "special treatment" is simply meeting a child where she is.

Framework: Addressing Bias and Building Trust at School

1

Name the bias — identify and document specific, concrete examples of differential treatment.

2

Engage directly — ask questions and clarify what she means rather than assuming or allowing withdrawal.

3

Offer choices — replace "no" or directives with two acceptable options to reduce power struggles.

4

Include proactively — ensure she has classroom roles, is in group photos, and receives warmth without having to earn it.

5

Provide extra support up front — accept that she needs more investment now to build trust over time.

6

Monitor and adjust — review progress regularly and shift strategies as her comfort grows.

How This Applies When she says "I'm sleepy," staff should engage rather than allow her to withdraw — asking what she needs and offering a choice of activity. She should receive hugs and praise without having to look sad first. Naming this pattern clearly in the school meeting is the starting point for change.

Practical Instructions

  • In the meeting, name the bias directly and give concrete examples — photos, missing roles, one-sided reporting.
  • Request that staff offer choices during transitions rather than directives or denials.
  • Ask that positive attention (praise, physical warmth) be given proactively, not only after she seeks it.
  • Propose a 2–4 week trial of consistent extra engagement and monitor whether her behavior shifts.

Assignment

Before the school meeting, write down three to five specific examples of exclusion or bias you have observed (photos, roles, report tone). Bring this list and your session notes to the meeting to anchor the conversation in facts. Afterward, track what changes — inclusion in activities, tone of reports, your daughter's mood after school — and bring your observations to the next session in 2–4 weeks.

Key Takeaways

Her struggles are responses to exclusion, not fixed character traits.
Naming bias clearly — with examples — is the first step toward changing it.
Choices reduce resistance; inclusion builds belonging.
Positive attention must be proactive, not earned through distress.
Extra support during transitions prevents longer-term difficulties.
If the environment doesn't shift, her difficulties will likely continue.

Bottom Line

Your daughter's school difficulties are not primarily behavioral — they are relational. Staff bias, whether intentional or not, has created an environment where she does not feel wanted, and her behavior reflects that. The path forward begins with naming what is happening clearly and advocating for the same baseline of warmth, inclusion, and engagement that every child deserves.

The upcoming school meeting is a concrete opportunity to shift this dynamic. Preparation, documentation, and a clear ask for a trial period of equitable support are your most effective tools. Progress will take weeks, not days — but consistent, warm engagement from staff can meaningfully change her experience at school.
