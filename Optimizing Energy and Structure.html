🧺 Optimizing Energy and Structure: Building Sustainable Routines with Narcolepsy

Meaningful projects deserve the energy that's actually available, not just whatever's left over

The Situation

Narcolepsy brings significant daytime fatigue that affects both productivity and motivation. Even with medication, tiredness persists and energy is hard to sustain across a full day. Work tasks get done reliably, driven by external incentive, but personal goals like sewing and house projects consistently lose out.

Daily tasks bring a real sense of overwhelm, decision-making around meals feels especially draining, and social connection has become harder to maintain — with a growing default toward doing everything alone.

Core Problem

Energy depletion and overwhelm are making it difficult to prioritize and complete anything beyond work. The current medication regimen isn't fully controlling fatigue, and there's a real mismatch between the desire to accomplish personal projects and the energy actually available, especially as the day wears on.

Decision fatigue compounds the problem, turning routine tasks like meal planning and cleaning into something burdensome. Social connection keeps getting deprioritized, which reinforces isolation over time.

"You only get 100 decisions a day... if you waste five on dinner, that could have gone to something else."

Understanding the Dynamics

Narcolepsy

A neurological disorder causing excessive daytime sleepiness and sudden sleep attacks — making energy a precious, carefully managed resource.

Decision Budget

Treating daily decision-making capacity as a literal, finite currency — every small choice spends from the same limited supply.

Self-Sufficiency as Isolation

A default toward handling everything alone that, over time, can quietly narrow a support network down to no one.

Externally vs. Internally Motivated Tasks

Work gets finished because it's incentivized from outside; personal passions need the same intentional structure to get the same follow-through.

Framework A

Energy Optimization

1
Prioritize high-energy tasks — like sewing or projects — earlier in the day, when medication is most effective.
2
Schedule routine tasks — like cleaning — for later, when energy is lower.
3
Limit repetitive chores to a set time, such as one hour of cleaning max.
4
Map out larger projects in advance, broken into smaller, manageable steps.
5
Accept incomplete tasks when necessary, and plan to revisit them when energy returns.
Applied Sewing is a genuine passion that often gets pushed to the end of the day, when energy is lowest. Moving it to a morning or early-evening slot, while medicated, allows real progress and a real sense of fulfillment.

Framework B

Decision Fatigue Reduction

1
Create a master list of go-to meals and routines.
2
Use the list to plan meals and grocery shopping, minimizing daily decision-making.
3
Automate or standardize repetitive choices wherever possible.
Applied Meals currently get "winged," leading to wasted energy and repetitive last-minute choices. A running list of favorites makes planning and shopping easier and frees up mental space for what actually needs a decision.

Framework C

Block Scheduling

1
Redo the daily schedule to reflect actual energy peaks and valleys.
2
Block out time for work, personal projects, cleaning, fitness, and relaxation.
3
Assign specific tasks to specific time blocks, prioritizing personal goals during high-energy periods.
Applied Instead of letting chores and projects pile up indefinitely, specific times get dedicated to them — weekends for projects, one hour daily for cleaning — with tasks broken into smaller, less daunting steps.

"You work your energy, you work with your energy."

Practical Instructions

  • Before starting a task, ask which time block it belongs in — if it doesn't fit the block you're in, note it for later instead of starting now.
  • Pick one small, low-stakes decision each day (what to wear, what route to take) and pre-decide it the night before to save that decision for the morning.
  • When a project gets left unfinished for the day, write down the exact next step before you stop, so restarting doesn't require re-deciding where you left off.
  • Once a week, notice one moment you defaulted to doing something alone that could have been shared or asked for help with.
  • At the end of each day, name one thing that got done during your peak energy window, separate from everything that didn't get to.

Assignment

Redo your daily schedule to align high-energy tasks like sewing with your medicated peak hours, and block out set time for cleaning, projects, and fitness. Build a running list of go-to meals to simplify planning and shopping, and choose one house project to break into steps for this weekend. Bring your schedule, meal list, and project notes to the next session.

Medication Note

Modafinil

Increase to 400mg (two 200mg tablets, morning) For wakefulness and sustained energy Monitor: headache, nausea, anxiety, insomnia

Stimulants (ER / IR)

Discuss increase to 15mg ER / 10mg IR with neurologist For continued wakefulness support Monitor: increased heart rate, jitteriness, insomnia, appetite changes Take only as prescribed

Key Takeaways

Energy, not just available time, should determine when a task gets scheduled.
Work gets finished reliably because it's externally incentivized — personal passions need the same intentional scheduling to get the same follow-through.
Every small decision draws from the same limited daily supply, whether it's spent on dinner or on something that matters more.
Leaving a project unfinished for the day is a plan, not a failure, when the next step is already written down.
A repeating, minimal cleaning window prevents chores from expanding to fill unlimited time.
Defaulting to self-sufficiency can quietly narrow a support network down to just yourself.
Medication timing and task timing work together — matching the two is part of the treatment plan, not separate from it.
Feeling accomplished comes from completing what was prioritized, not from completing everything.

Bottom Line

Narcolepsy makes energy a genuinely finite, precious resource. The problem isn't a lack of discipline or motivation — it's that meaningful personal activities have been getting whatever energy is left over, instead of the energy that's actually available.

Every choice, no matter how small, draws from the same limited daily capacity. Removing unnecessary ones — what to eat, when to clean — isn't a minor convenience. It's energy redirected toward things that actually matter.

Block scheduling and medication adjustments aren't about doing more. They're about making sure the things worth doing get the energy that's actually available for them — and accepting an unfinished task is part of that plan, not a departure from it.
