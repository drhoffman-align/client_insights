🛡️ Reclaiming Sanity at Work

Setting Boundaries and Protecting Yourself in Overwhelming Environments

The Situation

You are doing the work of several people and being evaluated as if you are one of them. The phones ring while someone is at the front desk. Insurance verifications stack up while you're scheduling. Confirmation calls go unmade because you're managing a patient who walked in unannounced. Every incomplete task at the end of the day feels like a personal failure — even though the math of what's being asked of you and what one person can actually do has never added up.

The anxiety isn't coming from you doing your job badly. It's coming from being placed in a structurally impossible position and then holding yourself responsible for the impossibility. That distinction is the starting point for everything in this session.

Core Problem

The central issue is a mismatch between what is being asked of you and what one person can actually do — compounded by the habit of internalizing that gap as personal failure. When things don't get done, the explanation you reach for is "I should have done more." The more accurate explanation is "the system does not have enough people to do this work."

The risk of the current pattern: you will burn out trying to meet expectations that were never designed to be met by one person, and when you eventually can't sustain it, you'll blame yourself for that too. The work here is to interrupt that pattern before it gets there — by clarifying what is actually yours to carry and what belongs somewhere else.

"You cannot do everything. Your priority is to yourself — to maintain your sanity."

Understanding the Dynamics

Overfunctioning

Taking on more than your fair share — often to compensate for systemic failures — in a way that is unsustainable and ultimately harms you. Overfunctioning feels like responsibility. It is actually an absorption of someone else's problem at your own expense.

Triage

The clinical practice of prioritizing where resources go when demand exceeds capacity. Not everything gets addressed immediately. Not everything can. Triage is not neglect — it is professional judgment about what matters most right now.

CYA (Cover Yourself)

Creating a written record of your actions and communications so that you have evidence if disputes arise about performance or expectations. This is not defensiveness — it is professional protection. It also shifts accountability back to where it belongs.

Paper Trail

Written documentation — emails, notes, records — that can demonstrate what you did, when you reported issues, and what management was told. Power in hostile or unreasonable work environments does not come from working harder. It comes from documentation.

A clear order for how to spend your capacity — when you can't do everything, this is the sequence

The anxiety of impossible demands gets managed by having a clear hierarchy. When you know the order — when you know what you will do first, second, and third regardless of what else is piling up — the overwhelm has somewhere to land. This is the order.

What Is Actually Yours — and What Is the System's

Your Responsibility

The patient or person physically in front of you
Doing your actual job to the best of your capacity
Reporting when you're over capacity or systems are down
Communicating your limits clearly and professionally
Protecting your own mental health

Not Your Responsibility

Compensating for understaffing
Doing the work of multiple roles for one person's pay
System failures and technology outages
Management's decisions about staffing levels
Outcomes that require more capacity than you were given

When You Feel Responsible for Everything

Pause and ask: is this a task that belongs in my column, or the system's? If the phone rings while you're with a patient, that's a staffing problem — not a failure of your attention. If insurance verification didn't happen because you were handling seven other things, that's a capacity problem. You can report it. You cannot fix it by trying harder.

"The way that you gain power is to maintain your professionalism and send emails — create a paper trail."

📄 Framework 2: CYA — Creating Your Paper Trail

Documentation as protection, accountability as power

In an unreasonable work environment, the most professional thing you can do is document everything. Not as evidence of complaint — as evidence of professional conduct. Every email you send reporting a capacity issue, every record of a system outage, every note about what was prioritized and why — these shift responsibility from you to the people who set the conditions.

1

Daily Summary Email

At the end of each shift, email your supervisor a brief summary: what was completed, what was not completed, and why. Keep it professional and factual.

Daily
2

Report System Failures Immediately

When a system is down or a tool fails, send an email: "I am unable to complete [task] today because [system] is unavailable. I'm notifying you so you can address this." The problem is now on their radar in writing.

As Needed
3

Forecast Problems in Advance

If you can see a problem coming — too many tasks, too little time — communicate before it becomes a crisis. "I want to flag that today's schedule makes it unlikely I can complete both X and Y. Please let me know which to prioritize."

Proactive
4

Invite Feedback — Then Set the Standard

Your initial email establishes the baseline. "Here's how I'm prioritizing. I'm open to suggestions." This invites their input while making your approach official. If they don't respond, you've documented that you tried.

One Time
5

Keep All Correspondence

Save every email. Do not rely on chat platforms that can be deleted. If something is said verbally that matters, follow up with an email: "Confirming our conversation today about..."

Always

The Initial Baseline Email — What to Send This Week

To:
[Supervisor Name(s)]
Subject:
Workload Overview and Task Prioritization

Hi [Name],

I wanted to give you a clear picture of how I've been managing my responsibilities over the past few months, and to share how I'm currently prioritizing tasks given the workload.

My daily responsibilities include [list 4–6 core tasks]. When all of these demand attention simultaneously — which is most days — I prioritize in this order: (1) patients who are physically present, (2) time-sensitive insurance and billing tasks, (3) scheduling and confirmation calls as capacity allows.

I want to be transparent that there are days when the volume of tasks exceeds what one person can reasonably complete in a shift. When that happens, I will document what was left undone and why, and I'll notify you directly.

I'm open to any suggestions you have for prioritizing or restructuring how I manage these tasks. I want to make sure we're aligned on expectations going forward.

Thank you,
[Your Name]

This email does four things: establishes your priority system, names the capacity reality, puts management on notice, and invites their input — all professionally.
Before — Absorbing It Silently

You try to do everything. Things get missed. You blame yourself. Management assumes you're managing fine. Nothing changes except your stress level.

After — Documenting It Professionally

You do what you can, report what you couldn't, and create a written record. Management is on notice. If problems continue, accountability is traceable — and it isn't pointing at you.

On the Power of the Paper Trail

If you are doing your job and documenting your work, there is very little they can do to you that doesn't leave a trail pointing back to the system's failures. If they ignore your documented concerns, the responsibility for what follows is theirs — in writing, with timestamps. The paper trail is not a complaint. It is professionalism. And professionalism, practiced consistently, is power in an unreasonable environment.

Putting It Into Practice

  • Start each day by reminding yourself of the priority order. Sanity first. Person in front of you second. Everything else: triage. When the day feels like it's spinning out, return to this sequence. It gives the overwhelm a structure to land in.

  • Stop apologizing internally for what you can't reach. When the phone rings while you're with someone, that is not your failure. When insurance calls don't get made because the front desk was full, that is a staffing decision, not a personal one. Name it accurately before your nervous system does.

  • Use email — not chat — for anything that matters. Chat can be deleted. Email cannot. If your supervisor gives you an instruction verbally, follow up in email to confirm. "Just confirming from our conversation today: I should prioritize X over Y when the schedule is full." That confirmation is now part of the record.

  • Send the initial baseline email this week. It sets the stage for everything else. Use the template from this document, adjust it to your specific tasks, and send it to whoever has authority over your workload. What you do after that — the daily summaries, the issue reports — builds on this foundation.

  • Continue building toward your backup plan. The medical billing and coding course is not just a plan B — it is a psychological asset. Knowing you have an exit that you're building toward changes how the current situation sits in your nervous system. Keep going. The current job is a position you're in, not one you're trapped in.

Key Takeaways

You are being asked to do the work of several people while being paid and evaluated as one. That is a systemic problem, not a personal failure. The math doesn't add up because it was never designed to.
Your primary job is to protect your own sanity. Not to absorb every task, not to compensate for understaffing, not to overfunc tion your way to the expectations set for a fully-staffed office. Sanity first.
Triage is professional judgment, not neglect. When you prioritize the person in front of you over the ringing phone, that is a sound clinical decision. Name it that way — to yourself and in your documentation.
The paper trail is your protection. Written documentation of what you completed, what you couldn't, and why — sent to management via email — shifts accountability back to the people who set the conditions. Your word plus a timestamp is more powerful than frustration alone.
Professionalism is power. Frame everything as information, not complaint. "I'm unable to complete X today due to Y" is a professional update. It puts management on notice without giving them grounds to respond punitively.
If they don't respond to your documented concerns, responsibility for what follows is theirs. You notified them. You documented your capacity. If problems escalate, the trail doesn't point at you.
Anything beyond your job description is not your job. The company's staffing decisions are their choices and their consequences. You are responsible for doing your job well — not for filling the roles they chose not to fill.
Building a backup plan is not disloyalty — it is sanity. The medical billing and coding course gives you options, and having options changes your relationship to this job from trapped to temporary. Both things matter.

🛡️ The Bottom Line

You are not failing at your job. You are being given a job that was designed to require more than one person, and you are holding yourself accountable for the parts that fall through the cracks. That accounting is wrong. The cracks are not yours. They belong to a system that chose to understaff and over-expect.

The two tools from this session — triage and documentation — work together. Triage clarifies what you can actually do. Documentation captures what you couldn't, and sends it back to the people who created the conditions. Together they protect your mental health, protect your job, and create a professional record that positions you correctly: as someone who showed up, did their work, communicated clearly, and reported what wasn't possible rather than silently absorbing it.

Your value is not measured by how much you can endure. The most professional thing you can do in this environment is refuse to over-function your way into burnout, maintain your sanity, keep building your options, and document everything. That is not weakness. That is self-protection — and in a workplace that asks too much, it is the only sustainable strategy.
