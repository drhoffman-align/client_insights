Alignment Psychiatry

Harnessing Trauma-Informed Leadership

Recognizing Power, Influence, and Authentic Connection

The Situation

You are navigating complex workplace dynamics around leadership, reputation, and how your influence is perceived by others. Your skills and insights are recognized, yet you have not actively sought formal advancement or recognition, creating a gap between what you offer and what you claim.

Running alongside this is a broader frustration with leadership structures that prioritize status over genuine understanding of people — a pattern that mirrors your own experience of being overlooked or mislabeled despite demonstrated effectiveness.

Core Problem

The central issue is a disconnect between your actual abilities and how you perceive — and assert — your own power. Past experiences around connection and validation appear to contribute to hesitance in asking for what you deserve.

Labels like "troublemaker" or "disruptor" have been applied to you in ways that obscure rather than reflect your genuine expertise and insight. Internalizing those labels compounds the gap between your real influence and your willingness to exercise it.

Understanding the Dynamics

Trauma-Informed Connection

Trauma can produce heightened sensitivity to others' needs and behaviors. When recognized as a skill, this awareness becomes a genuine interpersonal asset.

Brand vs. Reputation

Brand is the image you intentionally project; reputation is what colleagues actually believe about you. Both shape your access to influence and opportunity.

Disruptor / Truth Teller

Someone labeled a disruptor is often someone who sees and names truths others avoid — a form of leadership, not a liability.

Resource Proxy

Tangible assets like money often stand in for real ability or knowledge. Genuine skills and insight are resources in their own right — frequently more durable ones.

Framework 1 — Four Quadrants of Leadership

Maps leadership potential by combining ability with desire, clarifying why people end up in — or avoid — formal roles.

  • 1
    Wants to lead and can — the natural fit
  • 2
    Wants to lead but can't — desire without matching skill
  • 3
    Can lead but doesn't want to — common for high-ability individuals
  • 4
    Neither wants to lead nor can — often not placed in leadership
Application: Identifying which quadrant you occupy in a given context clarifies why you hold back and reveals where desire or intentionality could shift your trajectory.

Framework 2 — The Power Equation

Power = Resources + Brand + Reputation. All three components are active and can be developed independently.

  • 1
    Resources — skills, knowledge, relationships, and tangible assets
  • 2
    Brand — the intentional image and message you choose to project
  • 3
    Reputation — what others actually believe based on direct experience
Application: Your resources and reputation are already solid. The gap is brand — making your image more deliberate would bring all three components into alignment and expand your influence.

Practical Instructions

  • Identify which leadership quadrant applies to you in your current role and consider what that reveals about your choices.
  • Take inventory of your skills, knowledge, and connections as concrete resources — write them down.
  • Decide how you want to be perceived and choose one visible action this week that reflects that image.
  • When advancement or recognition is warranted, ask for it directly — waiting to be noticed is not a strategy.

Assignment

Before your next session, reflect on your current brand and reputation at work. List your key strengths and resources, write down how you believe colleagues currently perceive you, and identify one specific action to close the gap — such as sharing expertise in a meeting or volunteering for a visible project. Bring your notes to the next session.

Key Takeaways

1. Leadership needs both ability and desire

Understanding which you have — and which is missing — clarifies your options.

2. Trauma built real skills

Heightened empathy and forecasting are interpersonal assets, not deficits.

3. Brand is the missing piece

Your resources and reputation are strong; intentional branding completes the equation.

4. Disruptor is a descriptor, not a verdict

Naming truths others avoid is a form of leadership, even when it's uncomfortable.

5. Advocacy is required

Recognition rarely arrives uninvited — asking is part of the work.

6. Labels that don't fit shouldn't be internalized

How others characterize you is data, not identity.

Bottom Line

Your influence is real and already operating — the work now is to claim it deliberately. The gap between your abilities and your self-advocacy is not a character flaw; it has a history, and it can be addressed directly.

Moving forward, the focus is intentional branding and active advocacy. Reflect on how you are perceived, take steps to shape that image, and ask for what your skills and contributions have earned.
