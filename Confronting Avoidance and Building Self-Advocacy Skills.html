Breaking the Cycle of Avoidance

Building Self-Advocacy and Facing Reality  |  Alignment Psychiatry

The Situation

A long-sought job offer from DC government arrived at a salary below your current remote position, forcing a direct choice between career purpose and financial stability. Rather than engaging with the decision, the pull to delay and defer took over — a familiar pattern that has played out across finances, relationships, and academics.

These aren't isolated events. Ignoring bank statements, overdue rent, IRS correspondence, lost academic accommodations, and avoided relationship conversations all share the same root: avoidance as a default response to discomfort. ADHD adds real difficulty to follow-through, but the pattern runs deeper than executive function alone.

Core Problem

Avoidance provides immediate relief from anxiety but consistently produces the outcomes it was meant to prevent — financial penalties, missed opportunities, unresolved conflict, and accumulated shame. The temporary comfort of not acting is outweighed many times over by the long-term cost.

Shame from past avoidance makes future avoidance more likely, creating a self-reinforcing loop. People-pleasing and fear of conflict keep the loop running by making others' comfort feel more urgent than your own needs.

Understanding the Dynamics

AvoidanceChoosing not to face a problem or emotion — feels safe short-term but delays and typically worsens consequences.
Shame–Avoidance CycleAvoidance produces shame; shame makes future avoidance more likely, creating a loop that tightens over time.
People-PleasingPrioritizing others' comfort over your own needs — often mistaken for kindness, but it is a form of self-neglect.
Executive Function & ADHDADHD impairs planning, initiation, and follow-through — making avoidance easier and self-advocacy harder without deliberate supports.

Framework 1 — The Avoidance Cycle

  • 1A stressful situation arises (job offer, overdue bill, difficult conversation).
  • 2Anxiety, shame, or fear of conflict surfaces.
  • 3You delay, ignore, or withdraw from the issue.
  • 4Short-term relief follows — the discomfort temporarily subsides.
  • 5Consequences accumulate: missed opportunities, fees, relational damage.
  • 6Shame from those consequences reinforces avoidance in the next cycle.
How this applies: Delaying a response to the DC offer led to missed information about a better-suited role. Avoiding the leasing office led to late fees and legal risk that a single proactive email could have prevented. Each loop tightened the next.

Framework 2 — Self-Advocacy in Practice

  • 1Identify what you actually need or want in this situation.
  • 2Communicate that need directly — in writing if speaking feels too hard.
  • 3Accept that temporary discomfort or conflict is part of the process.
  • 4Act imperfectly — a flawed attempt is always better than inaction.
  • 5Reflect on what happened and what you would adjust next time.
How this applies: Reinstating ADHD accommodations requires initiating a new process — uncomfortable, but avoidance of it compounds academic stress. Writing down your needs before a difficult conversation reduces the chance people-pleasing takes over in the moment.

Practical Instructions

  • When you feel the urge to delay or ignore, pause and name it out loud: "This is avoidance."
  • Ask: "What am I protecting myself from, and is the avoidance actually preventing that harm?"
  • Take one small, concrete action — send the email, check the statement, make the call — even if imperfect.
  • Use external supports: calendar blocks, reminders, and written notes to lower the activation cost of hard tasks.

Assignment

Before the next session, identify one area where avoidance is currently active — financial, academic, or relational. Write down what you are avoiding and why, plan one specific action, do it, and briefly note the outcome and how you felt afterward. Bring that report to the next session.

Key Takeaways

Avoidance feels protective but reliably increases long-term harm.
Shame and avoidance reinforce each other — the cycle must be interrupted deliberately.
People-pleasing is self-neglect in disguise, not generosity.
ADHD raises the difficulty level; structured supports are not optional extras.
Waiting for the "right moment" or perfect readiness is itself avoidance.
Small, imperfect actions break the cycle — reflection turns them into learning.

Bottom Line

Avoidance has functioned as a shield, but it has not protected you — it has cost you money, opportunities, relationships, and self-respect. It is a learned defense, not a character flaw, and that means it can be unlearned. The work ahead is not about becoming fearless; it is about acting despite discomfort, one small step at a time.

ADHD and old patterns will create friction. That is expected. What matters is using structure, support, and self-compassion to keep moving forward. Your choices shape your reality — each time you act instead of avoid, you take back a measure of control over your own life.
