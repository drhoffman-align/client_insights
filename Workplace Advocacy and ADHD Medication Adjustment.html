Standing Up for Yourself in the Workplace

Navigating Medication Adjustment and Corporate Advocacy

The Situation

You are managing two overlapping challenges: an early-phase ADHD medication adjustment and a workplace conflict involving a policy-related incident that has drawn management scrutiny. Both require clear thinking and deliberate action at the same time.

At work, the core tension is that you are being questioned about an outcome that may not have been within your control. On the medication side, you are experiencing modest early benefits alongside some side effects, with dose optimization still ahead.

Core Problem

The primary pattern at play is internalizing responsibility for outcomes that belong to the system, not solely to you. Corporate environments often look for someone to absorb blame when processes fail, and without documentation and clear communication, that person is often the one who stays quiet.

Concurrently, an undertreated ADHD presentation makes it harder to track details, stay organized under pressure, and advocate confidently—which is exactly what this moment demands.

Understanding the Dynamics

Employment LawA body of law designed to protect employees from unfair treatment, retaliation, and wrongful termination. These protections exist and apply to you.
Reasonable AccommodationsEmployer-provided adjustments for employees with medical conditions. You can request these without disclosing a specific diagnosis.
Stimulant ToleranceThe brain can adapt to daily stimulant use, gradually reducing effectiveness. Strategic weekend breaks help preserve the medication's impact.
Initiation vs. Maintenance PhaseThe first 0–3 months on a new stimulant are an adjustment period; the maintenance phase begins once an optimal dose is established and stable.

Frameworks

1 · Corporate Self-Advocacy and Documentation

  1. 1Document all significant workplace interactions with time-stamped emails to yourself or relevant parties.
  2. 2Use professional, inquisitive language—ask clarifying questions rather than making accusations.
  3. 3Clearly state the facts: what you did, what policy applied, and what the actual outcome was.
  4. 4Highlight your contributions and institutional value to the organization.
  5. 5Acknowledge any genuine mistakes, then separately clarify whether those mistakes caused the problem.
  6. 6If relevant, note that a medical issue may affect your performance to open the door for accommodations.
  7. 7Frame all communication as seeking understanding and improvement, not as confrontation.
How This AppliesAfter any contentious meeting, send a brief recap email to your supervisor and copy yourself. This creates a record, demonstrates professionalism, and shapes the narrative with facts rather than impressions.

2 · Medication Adjustment and Maintenance

  1. 1Begin at 10 mg daily; trial 15 mg once or twice, then 20 mg if further improvement is needed.
  2. 2Take doses after breakfast and around lunch; never take after 5:00 PM to protect sleep.
  3. 3Skip doses on weekends when possible to reduce tolerance buildup.
  4. 4Track side effects daily: dry mouth, appetite changes, nausea, sleep disruption.
  5. 5Compare medicated versus non-medicated days to gauge real-world effectiveness.
How This AppliesYou are in the initiation phase. Feeling drained on unmedicated days is expected—your brain is adapting to new demands. Systematic tracking now gives your provider the data needed to dial in the right dose efficiently.

Practical Instructions

  • After any significant workplace interaction, send a factual recap email to yourself and, when appropriate, to your supervisor.
  • In all written communications, state facts clearly, acknowledge your role accurately, and ask questions rather than make charges.
  • If performance has been affected, you may note a medical issue to your employer without naming the diagnosis.
  • Continue the medication titration plan: 10 mg → 15 mg → 20 mg as tolerated; take with food, skip weekends, avoid late doses.

Assignment

Complete the communication rubric provided in session by answering the questions about your goals, feelings, and desired outcome. Use your answers to draft an email or letter addressing the workplace situation, following the documentation framework above. Optionally, run your draft through ChatGPT for tone refinement, then email the draft to your clinician for feedback before sending it to your employer. Aim to have a draft ready before your next appointment.

Key Takeaways

Document all significant workplace interactions in writing.
Professional, inquisitive language is more effective than confrontation.
State facts, acknowledge real mistakes, and separate them from outcomes.
Employment law exists to protect you—know that it applies here.
You can reference a medical issue to invite accommodations without naming a diagnosis.
Systematic dose tracking now leads to a better-fit medication plan later.

Bottom Line

You are dealing with two real, concurrent pressures—and both are manageable with structured action. At work, your most effective tool is clear, documented, professional communication that keeps the focus on facts rather than blame. Your institutional knowledge and track record are genuine assets worth naming.

On the medication side, you are still early in the process. The discomfort you feel on adjustment days is part of the initiation phase, not a signal that treatment isn't working. Complete the rubric, send the draft, and bring your questions to the next session. These steps are concrete and within reach.
