🦉 Learning to Choose Yourself: Breaking the Cycle of Over-Responsibility and Embracing Rest

Redirecting the same advocacy given to everyone else toward her own well-being

The Situation

Work currently feels overwhelming, with a constant, hypervigilant sense of responsibility for outcomes beyond her control. Communities she feels genuinely compelled to help also leave her feeling taken advantage of, and a new "maxi flex" schedule meant to offer flexibility has instead produced longer hours.

Boundaries are hard to hold, and the default is to prioritize others' needs. There's real pressure to make everything look composed despite ongoing crises underneath — and real conflict about whether taking FMLA is "justified," given that she can still function socially and maintain appearances.

A history of over-functioning and perfectionism in professional settings, past traumatic experiences in graduate school, and a learned discomfort with accepting rest as legitimate all sit underneath the current moment — alongside a tendency to compartmentalize work, family, and social life, and a support system that doesn't always fully understand what's going on.

Core Problem

A chronic pattern of prioritizing others' needs and external demands over her own well-being has produced real exhaustion, resentment, and a lack of genuine rest. Decisions that are actually for herself are rare; the default is whatever benefits others or maintains the appearance of having it together.

There's a clear mismatch between how she appears — competent, high-achieving, a "rock star" — and how she actually feels: overwhelmed, unsafe, depleted. This pattern was learned early and has been reinforced by both professional and family dynamics ever since.

"You almost always choose others."

Understanding the Dynamics

Hypervigilance

A state of constant alertness, often built by chronic stress or trauma — always anticipating problems, always feeling responsible for preventing them.

Boundaries

Limits that protect time, energy, and well-being — especially difficult to hold at work, where the cost of holding them can feel high.

FMLA

A legal provision allowing job-protected leave for medical or family reasons — reframed here as a tool for genuine healing, not just a last resort.

Psychological Safety

Feeling mentally and emotionally protected from harm — distinct from appearing functional, and currently missing even while functioning looks intact.

Minority Tax

The extra burden placed on someone from an underrepresented group to represent or advocate for their community, on top of their actual job.

Framework A

The "Choosing Yourself" Framework

1
Notice the decision point — recognize when a choice is forming between your needs and someone else's.
2
Pause and reflect — ask, "Who is this decision really for?"
3
Assess safety and needs — what would actually feel safe and cared for right now?
4
Experiment with choosing yourself — even when it feels uncomfortable or unfamiliar.
5
Observe the outcome — notice what actually happens, internally and externally.
Applied Reluctance to take FMLA, and difficulty setting boundaries with colleagues, both trace back to a default of choosing others — like working extra hours specifically to "make things look good" for the team. The superhero who always falls on the sword is admirable and, eventually, unsustainable.

Framework B

The "Rest and Integration" Model

1
Acknowledge the need for rest — not a luxury, a necessity for healing.
2
Create protected time — tools like FMLA can carve out real, uninterrupted space.
3
Allow for discomfort — rest will likely feel strange or "unproductive" at first.
4
Engage in reflection — use the time to process and reconnect with yourself.
5
Integrate what you learn — bring it gradually into daily life.
Applied Previous time off was unplanned and never allowed for true rest or therapeutic work. Planned, intentional rest — supported by therapy — is what could actually interrupt the burnout cycle instead of just pausing it temporarily.
Before Works through every crisis, never fully rests, returns to old patterns
→
After With intentional rest and reflection, learns boundaries and sustains healthier habits
"Only you can provide the protection you need, because only you know what you need, when you need it."

Practical Instructions

  • Before agreeing to extra hours "to make things look good," name specifically who benefits from that decision.
  • Track one physical sensation — jaw, shoulders, stomach — that shows up during hypervigilant moments, and note when it eases.
  • When guilt shows up about resting, write down what the guilt is actually protecting you from, rather than obeying it.
  • Practice saying "let me check and get back to you" before automatically agreeing to a new request.
  • Notice one moment this week where appearance and reality matched, and one where they didn't, without judging either.

Assignment

Take an inventory of how you advocate for others at work — fighting for resources, protecting their time, ensuring their needs are met. Write a brief description of "work self": her qualities, strategies, and motivations. Reflect on what it would look like to apply that same advocacy to yourself. Key Takeaways

A flexible schedule intended to reduce hours can quietly expand them instead, without it ever feeling like a decision was made.
Advocacy skills used constantly for other people have never been pointed toward herself with the same intensity.
Unplanned time off in the past didn't function as rest because nothing about it was actually protected or intentional.
Feeling responsible for a community's outcomes and being personally responsible for them are not the same thing.
A nervous system can register threat even when the external situation looks entirely manageable from the outside.
Guilt about taking FMLA is tracking a belief about worthiness, not an accurate read of medical necessity.
The discomfort of unstructured rest is an expected part of the adjustment, not evidence that rest isn't needed.
Appearing composed and actually being okay are separate facts that can diverge for a long time before either gets addressed.

Bottom Line

Competence has made real depletion easy to miss — by colleagues, by family, and by herself — because the outward signs of struggle keep getting absorbed into an already-high baseline of functioning. Looking fine and being fine have quietly become the same thing on paper, without actually being the same thing underneath.

Rest hasn't worked as rest so far because it's never been protected or planned. An unplanned pause just delays the same pattern; it doesn't interrupt it. That distinction is what makes FMLA, used intentionally, different from simply taking time off.

Choosing herself requires the same deliberate advocacy already directed at other people's needs, redirected inward — starting with treating her own well-being as a case worth making, rather than a request that needs an apology attached to it.
