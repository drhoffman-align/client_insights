🎈 Letting Go of "Should": Acceptance, Boundaries, and Self-Protection

Wishing someone would act differently has never once made them act differently

The Situation

Repeated boundary violations, especially from a father, bring on frustration, hurt, and a sense of powerlessness when needs go unmet despite being clearly communicated. The emotional fallout of feeling unloved or uncared for by important people keeps resurfacing.

A history of trauma and a pattern of shrinking or minimizing personal needs runs underneath this, likely linked to borderline personality disorder and longstanding fears of abandonment. High sensitivity to others' emotions often shows up as feeling responsible for managing or fixing relational dynamics that aren't actually one person's to fix.

Core Problem

An expectation that others "should" behave differently — respecting boundaries, loving in the way needed, acting logically or caringly — sets up a collision with reality every time it goes unmet. That collision produces hurt and a sense of invalidation, over and over.

The mismatch is between wishes for how people should act and the reality of their actual motivations and behavior. This repeated pattern makes it difficult to accept people as they are and to protect against the disappointment that follows when they aren't.

"Should, could, and would are the biggest lies in the world. They're wishes, not reality."

Understanding the Dynamics

Defense Mechanisms

When people act contrary to what they know is right, it's often a way of protecting themselves emotionally — not a decision made against you specifically.

Acceptance

Letting go of the wish that someone will change, and instead accepting them as they are. Not giving up — making peace with what is, in order to protect yourself.

Borderline Personality Disorder

Includes heightened sensitivity to abandonment and a tendency to minimize one's own needs to preserve a relationship.

Boundary Violation Responsibility

Once a boundary has been clearly stated, continuing to cross it shifts responsibility onto the other person — it's no longer a matter of them not knowing.

Emotional Caretaking

Feeling responsible for managing or smoothing over other people's emotions, often as a learned response to a sensitive nervous system and past instability.

Framework A

The Limits of "Should, Could, Would"

1
Recognize that "should," "could," and "would" are wishes, not realities.
2
Observe the evidence of how people actually behave, not how you wish they would.
3
Accept that people act according to their own motivations and training, not your expectations.
4
Adjust your expectations to align with reality, not wishes.
Applied A father acting from his own training and self-interest — not malice, not absence of love — is closer to doctors who "should" wash their hands but sometimes don't. Wishing it were otherwise has never changed the pattern.

Framework B

The Bell Curve of Truth

1
Understand that "truth" isn't black and white — it's a bell curve of what's reasonable.
2
Notice that most people sit in the middle, doing what's reasonable for them, not necessarily what's ideal for you.
3
Recognize outliers exist, but the majority act in self-protective, self-centered ways.
Applied This isn't a personal failing or a uniquely unfair situation — it's a broader human pattern. Most people fall in the reasonable-for-them middle, not at the ideal end being hoped for.
Harmful outliers Reasonable-for-them majority Exceptionally giving outliers

Framework C

Self-Protection and Centering Your Needs

1
Notice when others are acting in self-protective ways.
2
Redirect focus from trying to change them to protecting yourself.
3
Make decisions based on your own needs and well-being.
4
Reserve your energy for what matters to you.
Applied Energy has gone toward trying to get someone to do something he was never going to do. Accepting that his default is self-protection frees that energy up for needs that are actually within reach.

Practical Instructions

  • Before repeating a request you've already made clearly, ask whether you're informing them or hoping repetition will finally make it land.
  • When you catch yourself explaining why someone "should" act differently, rewrite the sentence starting with what they actually did instead.
  • Keep a running note of which relationships give back in kind, and let that list — not hope — guide where your energy goes next.
  • After a boundary gets crossed a second time, decide in advance what you'll do differently, rather than deciding in the moment while upset.
  • When you feel responsible for managing someone else's reaction, pause and ask whose feelings you're actually trying to protect.

Assignment

Look up definitions of "acceptance" and "forgiveness" from a dictionary or psychology source, review the therapist's email on the topic, and reflect on how each definition applies to your own relationships. Come ready to discuss next session.

Key Takeaways

A boundary you've clearly stated shifts responsibility onto the other person the moment it's crossed again.
Most people are operating in the reasonable-for-them middle of the curve, not at the ideal end you're hoping for.
A parent's failure to show up in the way you need is more often about their own limits than about your worth.
Repeated disappointment is often a sign of measuring people against a wish rather than their actual pattern.
Feeling responsible for smoothing over other people's emotions is a learned habit, not a requirement.
Acceptance and resignation aren't the same thing — one still involves choosing how to respond.
Energy spent trying to get someone to become who they've never been is energy unavailable for your own life.
Sensitivity to others and a history of trauma make acceptance harder to practice, not impossible to reach.

Bottom Line

The pain here isn't coming from being unreasonable. It's coming from holding people to a version of them that exists only as a wish — and the gap between that wish and their actual, observable pattern is what keeps producing hurt.

Most people default to protecting themselves, not out of malice directed at any one person. That means repeated boundary crossing was never really a referendum on worth. It was a demonstration of someone else's default setting, playing out the same way it plays out with everyone else.

Adjusting expectations to match reality isn't lowering the standard. It's redirecting the energy that's been spent trying to change someone else's default into decisions and relationships that actually meet the standard being asked for all along.
