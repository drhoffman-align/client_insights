🧭 Finding Fulfillment and Purpose at Work: Building Systems, Managing Energy, and Redefining Success

Doing things well and feeling like they matter are two different achievements

The Situation

A recent move and a period of professional uncertainty have led into a new, demanding role as an Internal Control Administrator at a federal agency — working in a cubicle-based, distraction-prone environment with a small, overextended team. Building an internal control evaluation plan for the agency is both daunting and unprecedented in this workplace; no existing template or system exists to lean on.

Multiple personal and professional obligations — managing a rental property, family responsibilities, an entirely new job — are running at once, leaving a scattered, drained feeling. The goal isn't just performing well. It's finding genuine purpose in the work itself, beyond financial incentive.

Core Problem

A strong drive for excellence hasn't translated into fulfillment. The complexity and novelty of the new role, the absence of existing systems, and the pressure to do more with less are producing overwhelm rather than the sense of purpose being sought.

There's also a habit of feeling that she should be acting rather than taking time to think and plan — as if organizing her thoughts is a delay rather than part of the work. The result is a mismatch between wanting meaningful work and the reality of bureaucratic demands and unclear expectations.

"Everything that matters has energy related to it."

Understanding the Dynamics

Reasonable Accommodation

Workplace adjustments — noise-canceling headphones, telework, flexible scheduling — that support sustained performance. A legitimate tool, not a sign of falling behind.

Role Ambiguity

The strain of standing between two stakeholder groups with different expectations — here, described as being "the meat in the sandwich" between auditors and end users.

The Action Bias

The pull to feel productive only when visibly "doing" something, which crowds out the thinking and planning that complex work actually requires.

Assumed Understanding

The gap between what's said and what's actually heard — the source of costly miscommunication when instructions or expectations go unconfirmed.

Framework A

Fulfillment vs. Excellence

1
Define excellence — the desire to do things well.
2
Recognize the relationship — pursuing excellence can lead to fulfillment, but the two aren't the same thing.
3
Identify what brings fulfillment — energy, enjoyment, alignment with purpose.
Applied Strong performance ratings have been earned consistently, but they haven't come with a matching sense of satisfaction or purpose — a clear sign excellence has been chased while fulfillment was left unexamined.

Framework B

Relationship Energy Mapping

1
View engagements as relationships — work, family, and projects each either add to or drain your energy.
2
Assess each one — do you feel energized or depleted after engaging with it?
3
Prioritize what's net positive — and reduce time spent on what consistently isn't.
Applied Energy is currently spread across a new job, a rental property, and family responsibilities. Mapping where it actually goes clarifies why depletion is happening and where to start pulling back.

Framework C

Systems Thinking and Mind Mapping

1
Map the roles and processes in your work — auditor, end user, internal control admin.
2
Visualize the connections to clarify your place and impact.
3
Practice writing and drawing these maps to solidify understanding.
Applied Organizing complex, unfamiliar tasks has been difficult without a system in place. Drawing out how auditors, end users, and this role actually connect turns an abstract job description into something that can be planned around.

Framework D

Energy Optimization

1
Identify peak energy times during the day.
2
Schedule meaningful work during those peak periods.
3
Delegate or defer less important or draining tasks to lower-energy windows.
Applied Best energy has often gone toward urgent-but-less-meaningful tasks. Shifting the internal control evaluation plan work to peak-energy hours protects it from being squeezed out by whatever feels most urgent that day.

Practical Instructions

  • Before agreeing to a new task, ask which role you're being asked to play in that moment — auditor, admin, or end user — so it doesn't blur into everything else on your plate.
  • Block 15 minutes at the start of the evaluation plan project to sketch its shape before writing a single line of it.
  • When you catch yourself feeling guilty for sitting and thinking, name it out loud as "planning," not "stalling."
  • Ask the person requesting something to restate it back in their own words before you commit to a deadline.
  • At the end of each week, note one relationship or task you handed off, even partially, and what that freed up.

Assignment

Sketch a mind map of your work responsibilities and relationships — auditor, end user, internal control admin, resources, mission — using pen and paper, labeling each part and your specific duties. Complete before the next session after vacation, targeting September 2nd.

Key Takeaways

Good performance ratings and genuine fulfillment are not automatically the same thing.
Feeling scattered often comes from energy spread across too many active commitments, not from any single one being too demanding.
A new, undefined role can be clarified on paper before it's clarified in practice.
Sitting still to think is a form of productive work, not a delay of it.
Peak-energy hours are a limited resource worth protecting for the work that matters most.
Workplace accommodations are a legitimate tool for sustainable performance, not a sign of struggling to keep up.
Miscommunication often comes from assuming a shared understanding that was never actually confirmed.
Standing between two stakeholder groups with different expectations is a structural position, not a personal failing.

Bottom Line

Excellence has been the variable getting optimized — and it's worked, in the sense that the performance ratings show it. But fulfillment comes from a different source entirely: energy, enjoyment, alignment with purpose. Doing the same things more skillfully was never going to produce it, because it was never the thing being measured.

Feeling scattered isn't a personal deficiency. It's the predictable result of an undefined role, several competing obligations, and no system yet built to hold any of it. The frameworks introduced here aren't extra polish on top of the job — they're the missing infrastructure the role hasn't had until now.

The work itself connects to something real: making sure an agency's resources, and by extension its mission, are properly accounted for. Building the systems to do that well, protecting the energy it takes, and giving thinking the same legitimacy as action is what will let that connection actually be felt, instead of staying buried under logistics.
