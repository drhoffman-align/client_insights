Owning Your Power

Navigating Leadership, Uncertainty, and Advocacy in Organizational Change

The Situation

You lead a life sciences team inside a company that has recently been acquired. New ownership has set a 50% revenue increase as the target for the coming year, with little guidance on how to reach it and no commitment to additional resources. Your team looks to you for answers, but the information and authority needed to provide them are often out of reach.

Your direct supervisor, formerly the business owner, now serves as a go-between but lacks subject matter expertise and tends to overpromise while blocking direct access to upper management. This is happening alongside personal stressors — an upcoming surgery, a recent move, and family transitions — that add to an already demanding load.

Core Problem

The central issue is a mismatch: the organization expects significant growth but has not provided the resources, information, or structural support to make it achievable. You are positioned between unrealistic targets from above and a team that needs real answers and advocacy from you.

Your supervisor functions as a barrier rather than a bridge — limiting communication, overpromising to leadership, and leaving you with the pressure of filling those gaps. This creates a cycle of frustration and undervaluation that will continue unless you take deliberate action to assert your influence and reframe how your role is understood.

Understanding the Dynamics

Power Moves Specific actions you can take to assert your influence — presenting data, setting boundaries, or escalating issues directly to decision-makers.
Gatekeeping When someone controls access to information or decision-makers to preserve their own standing, leaving others without what they need to lead.
Realistic Expectations Goals calibrated to actual resources and capacity — protecting your team from burnout and protecting quality from the pressure of overpromising.
Advocacy Actively representing your team's needs to leadership, especially when those above you are disconnected from operational realities.

Frameworks

1 — Power and Influence in Leadership

  • 1Identify what you actually control, what you influence, and what you can leverage.
  • 2Define your specific "power moves" for this situation.
  • 3Assess the right timing and context for each move.
  • 4Communicate needs and boundaries clearly to both your team and upper management.
  • 5Evaluate outcomes and adjust your approach as needed.
How This Applies

Before this session, leaving felt like your only option. You now have a menu of moves: direct budget presentations, defined resource requirements, and strategic escalation. Your expertise gives you leverage others in the organization do not have.

2 — Aligning Expectations with Resources

  • 1Quantify the gap between the stated revenue target and current resources.
  • 2Build a data-driven case using budgets, utilization rates, and growth margins.
  • 3Present the gap to decision-makers and advocate for adjustments.
  • 4Set and communicate honest expectations with your team to prevent attrition.
How This Applies

A 50% growth target without a resource plan is not a strategy — it is a pressure campaign. Naming the gap with data shifts the conversation from vague pressure to concrete problem-solving, and positions you as a solution-oriented leader rather than a resistor.

Practical Instructions

  • Write out your list of power moves and the scenarios in which each one is appropriate.
  • When communication is blocked, seek alternative channels or document your requests in writing.
  • Use concrete data — not just frustration — when making resource requests to leadership.
  • Monitor your team's morale and intervene early if signs of burnout or dissatisfaction appear.

Assignment

Before the next session, write out your power moves and realistic scenarios for using each one. Decide on your leadership goal: do you want a broader decision-making role in the organization, or do you want to focus tightly on leading your department? Gather data on the resource gap and prepare talking points for a direct conversation with upper management. Bring your list, your goal reflection, and any data you have gathered.

Key Takeaways

Your expertise is irreplaceable — no one else in the organization can do what you do for this department.
The expectation-resource gap is real and must be named explicitly, not absorbed quietly.
Your supervisor's gatekeeping is a problem to route around, not a wall to stop at.
Data-driven advocacy is more durable than frustration-driven pushback.
You have multiple levers of influence — leaving is one option, not the only one.
Clarity about your leadership goals will shape which power moves make the most sense.

Bottom Line

This session surfaced a clear truth: your department depends on you in ways the organization has not fully acknowledged, and that dependency is itself a form of power. Recognizing it — and acting from it deliberately — changes your position from someone absorbing pressure to someone shaping outcomes.

The path forward requires defining what you want, naming what you need, and communicating both with data and directness. The uncertainty in this organization is real, but so is your ability to influence how it resolves. The next steps are concrete: list your moves, clarify your goal, and prepare to make your case.
