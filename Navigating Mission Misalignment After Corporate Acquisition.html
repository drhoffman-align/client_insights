Alignment Psychiatry

Navigating Uncertainty and Mission Misalignment After Organizational Change

Therapeutic insights to support self-advocacy and informed decision-making during transition

The Situation

A recent company acquisition has significantly disrupted a previously clear sense of purpose, direction, and role. The shift happened quickly, leaving limited time to adjust before the environment changed around her. Relationships with decision-makers are less defined, and the nature of her contributions feels different than before.

An upcoming debrief meeting with her supervisor adds pressure. She is uncertain how openly to express her concerns, particularly given that she is privately weighing whether to stay with the organization long-term.

Core Problem

The central difficulty is mission misalignment paired with a loss of agency. The new organization's emphasis on profitability and leadership training does not match her value-driven, impact-oriented work identity. Her skills are being utilized in a transactional way rather than as a core part of a shared purpose.

This mismatch is producing low motivation, confusion about her future role, and reluctance to speak up — partly out of concern that voicing dissatisfaction will label her as a flight risk before she has enough information to make a decision.

Understanding the Dynamics

Mission Misalignment

A gap between personal values and the organization's stated direction. Here, her drive for meaningful impact conflicts with the new focus on profit and training infrastructure.

Uncertainty Tolerance

The capacity to function when outcomes are unclear. Her uncertainty has shifted from low to very high, making sustained motivation difficult to maintain.

Flight Risk Perception

The concern that expressing dissatisfaction signals intent to leave — which may cause leadership to disinvest before she has clarity about her options.

Transactional vs. Purposeful Use of Expertise

Her skills are currently deployed as a resource rather than as a driving force, which erodes engagement and a sense of professional ownership.

Framework 1 — "Stating the Obvious" Communication

  1. 1Identify the specific gap or change that needs to be addressed.
  2. 2State observable facts without attaching blame or emotional language.
  3. 3Describe how the change has affected your role or understanding.
  4. 4Ask direct clarifying questions to fill in knowledge gaps.
  5. 5Withhold sensitive intentions (e.g., considering other opportunities) until you have more information.
How it applies: Instead of "I'm unhappy and may leave," say: "Before the acquisition, I had a clear direction — now there's significant uncertainty about my role. Can you help me understand what's planned?" This opens a productive dialogue without overexposing her position.

Framework 2 — "Three Outcomes of Feedback" Model

  1. 1Raise a concern or name an impact plainly: "My role has changed in ways I didn't anticipate."
  2. 2Observe the response — acknowledgment and action, acknowledgment without repair, or dismissal.
  3. 3Use that response to calibrate how much trust and openness is warranted going forward.
How it applies: Feedback is information-gathering, not just problem-solving. How leadership responds to her concerns will tell her as much about the organization as any formal answer will. Like telling someone they've stepped on your foot — their reaction reveals whether it was accidental or not.

Practical Instructions

  • Before the meeting, list the key changes you've observed and draft 2–3 neutral, fact-based questions or statements.
  • During the meeting, use observable language — describe what changed, ask what's planned, and request clarity on your role.
  • Do not disclose job search activity or frame your concerns as ultimatums.
  • After the meeting, record what was said and how your supervisor responded — this becomes data for your decisions.

Assignment

Revise your meeting notes to include both process observations and "stating the obvious" statements about the changes you've experienced. In the meeting, raise at least one observable change and ask for direct clarification about your role and the company's direction. Afterward, write a brief reflection on how leadership responded and what that tells you. Bring your updated notes and meeting summary to the next session in two weeks.

Key Takeaways

Mission misalignment — not performance — is the primary driver of low motivation here.

Stating observable facts is safe, professional, and effective for opening dialogue.

Asking for clarity is not the same as signaling intent to leave.

How leadership responds to feedback is itself meaningful information.

Uncertainty is temporary — gathering information reduces it.

The goal of the meeting is information, not resolution — decisions can come later.

Bottom Line

This is a period of genuine disruption, and the discomfort she's experiencing — disappointment, confusion, reduced motivation — is a proportionate response to real change, not a sign that something is wrong with her. The frameworks from this session give her a structured, low-risk way to engage with leadership and learn what she needs to know before making any decisions.

The immediate priority is entering the meeting with a clear, neutral agenda: understand her role, assess the organization's direction, and gather data. Whatever she learns will inform her next step — whether that is investing further in this organization or preparing to move on. Both are legitimate outcomes.
