Finding Focus and Managing Anxiety

ADHD, Social Performance, and Medication Strategies

The Situation

You were first diagnosed with ADHD in 2015 and have tried both Strattera and Adderall in the past, stopping each due to side effects or a preference to avoid daily medication. Recent increases in work demands have brought the question of medication back to the table, now with a clearer rationale for consistent use rather than situational dosing.

Anxiety has also played a significant role, particularly in high-pressure or client-facing environments, showing up as social anxiety, fear of making mistakes, sleep and appetite disruption, and occasional withdrawal. You are currently in a less demanding role and functioning adequately, while also pursuing certification for anxiety to support a student loan discharge application.

Core Problem

ADHD and anxiety reinforce each other in a cycle: executive dysfunction creates fear of falling behind, and that fear makes focus and task initiation even harder. The result is a pattern where high pressure is sometimes needed just to get started — and when that pressure tips over into overwhelm, functioning stalls entirely.

Compartmentalization is a key challenge. When one stressor dominates your attention, other demands pile up unmanaged. The gap between how much effort things require internally and what others see externally can feel isolating and hard to explain.

Understanding the Dynamics

Executive Dysfunction

Impaired ability to initiate, organize, and complete tasks; a core feature of ADHD rooted in frontal lobe development.

Anxiety vs. Panic Attacks

Panic attacks are brief and intense with physical symptoms; anxiety attacks are lower-level but can persist much longer and go unrecognized.

Compartmentalization

The capacity to mentally set aside one stressor to focus on another — difficult with ADHD, leading to paralysis when multiple demands compete.

Stimulant vs. Non-Stimulant Medications

Stimulants act quickly on focus and executive function; non-stimulants are slower to work and are sometimes preferred when anxiety is prominent.

Frameworks

1 — Anxiety as Energy

  • 1Recognize anxiety as the body preparing for performance, not a malfunction.
  • 2Channel that activation into preparation and action before it becomes avoidance.
  • 3Distinguish motivating anxiety (useful) from paralyzing anxiety (needs intervention).
How it applies: Reframing workplace anxiety as useful energy reduces the shame around it and opens the door to using it constructively rather than fighting it.

2 — ADHD Neurodevelopmental Model

  • 1Acknowledge that ADHD brains require more stimulation or urgency to initiate tasks.
  • 2Recognize deadline-dependence as a symptom, not a character flaw.
  • 3Use daily medication and structure to build a reliable baseline — a "new normal" for focus.
How it applies: This model explains the lifelong pattern of last-minute starts and validates the need for consistent treatment rather than relying on urgency or willpower alone.

Practical Instructions

  • Take Vyvanse 30mg each morning consistently — daily use, not situational, is the goal.
  • Eat regularly even if appetite is reduced; use smoothies or protein shakes around meal times.
  • Monitor for side effects (sleep disruption, jitteriness, anxiety changes) and report them promptly.
  • When anxiety rises, treat it as a cue to prepare and act — not a signal to withdraw or avoid.

Assignment

Begin Vyvanse as prescribed and keep a brief daily log of focus, energy, appetite, and any side effects. Research the specific certification documentation required for the student loan discharge application and send that information before the next appointment. Update your insurance information on the therapy platform. Come to the two-week follow-up ready to review medication response and next steps for documentation.

Key Takeaways

ADHD and anxiety form a cycle — each one amplifies the other.

Anxiety can be motivating; the goal is direction, not elimination.

Daily medication builds a stable baseline that situational dosing cannot.

Vyvanse was chosen for its smoother profile and lower risk of worsening anxiety.

Appetite suppression is expected — plan nutrition proactively, don't skip meals.

Consistent follow-up allows timely dose adjustments and documentation support.

This session clarified the relationship between ADHD and anxiety in your daily functioning and established a treatment direction grounded in consistency rather than crisis response. Beginning daily Vyvanse, tracking its effects, and maintaining regular nutrition are the immediate priorities.

Open communication between appointments — about medication, side effects, or logistical needs like documentation — keeps treatment responsive. The goal is not just managing symptoms in difficult moments, but building a reliable baseline that makes effective functioning the default.
