Finding Your Flow: ADHD, Anxiety & Identity in High-Pressure Environments

A personalized therapeutic insights document to support your progress between sessions.

The Situation

You are a high-performing professional with a longstanding history of ADHD—showing up as procrastination, distractibility, difficulty with motivation, and overstimulation. You currently take Strattera and have tried stimulant medications before with mixed results. Your work environment is fast-paced and high-stakes, generating significant stress, particularly around performance and communicating with senior leadership.

You also experience anxiety in social situations and carry a habitual pattern of self-criticism that has become a default motivator. You are proactive about seeking support and have a solid history of self-awareness—both of which are real assets as we work through this together.

Core Problem

ADHD and anxiety are feeding each other in a cycle. ADHD creates difficulties with focus, motivation, and task-switching; anxiety amplifies those difficulties by fueling self-doubt, overthinking, and an expectation of failure before you even begin. The result is a reliance on stress and shame as fuel—which works, but at a high emotional cost and is not sustainable.

A key mismatch exists between your actual performance—which others respect—and your internal narrative, which is far more critical. Closing that gap is central to the work ahead.

Understanding the Dynamics

ADHD & Anxiety OverlapADHD creates focus and motivation challenges; anxiety magnifies them by adding self-doubt and rumination on top.
Stress-Driven MotivationUsing anxiety or shame to push through procrastination works short-term but reinforces anxiety as a required condition for action.
Imposter SyndromeFeeling like a fraud despite a solid track record—especially prominent in high-visibility roles where external scrutiny feels amplified.
OverstimulationToo much sensory or cognitive input triggers overwhelm, heightens anxiety, and creates a strong need for quiet recovery time.

Framework 1

Input → Processing → Output Model of Communication

1
Input: Receive information from others.
2
Processing: Organize and think through your response internally.
3
Output: Express your thoughts verbally.
How this applies to you: Your challenge is not comprehension—it's the gap between processing and output under pressure. Anxiety interrupts that transition, causing over-explanation or self-correction mid-sentence. Knowing the delay is in step 3 lets you apply targeted tools: pause, breathe, then speak. Use "Does that make sense?" as a simple check-in rather than a sign of uncertainty.

Framework 2

Identity vs. Brand vs. Reputation

1
Identity: Who you are at your core—stable, internal, yours alone.
2
Brand: The professional persona you project in a given context.
3
Reputation: What others have come to know you for over time.
How this applies to you: When your sense of self is anchored to brand or reputation, others' opinions and high-pressure moments feel existential. Grounding in your core identity creates stability that external feedback cannot shake. Your capabilities and expertise are real—your internal narrative simply hasn't caught up to that yet.

Practical Instructions

  • Take Strattera (25mg) and Vyvanse (10mg) together first thing in the morning; avoid caffeine for the first week.
  • In meetings, resist the urge to over-explain—pause, then ask "Does that make sense?" and let others respond.
  • Notice when anxiety or shame is pushing you to act; try setting one small, intentional goal instead before defaulting to stress.
  • Use brief morning breathwork and journaling to reduce overstimulation before the day begins.

Assignment

For the next two weeks, take both medications each morning as directed and keep a short daily log. Note your focus and motivation level, any anxiety or overstimulation, physical side effects, and one notable experience from a meeting or high-pressure moment. Avoid caffeine in week one; reintroduce slowly if needed. Bring this journal to your next session so we can assess the medication combination and refine what's working.

Key Takeaways

ADHD and anxiety are intertwined—addressing both together is essential.
Stress and shame as motivators work short-term but are emotionally costly and unsustainable.
Your communication difficulty is about self-interruption under pressure, not comprehension.
Others respect your contributions more than your internal narrative currently reflects.
Identity is separate from brand and reputation—grounding there reduces anxiety significantly.
Self-compassion and intentional planning can gradually replace anxiety as your primary driver.

Bottom Line

This session clarified how ADHD and anxiety reinforce each other in your day-to-day life, particularly at work. The patterns—stress-driven motivation, self-criticism, identity tied to external performance—make sense given your history, but they carry real costs. The work ahead is about building sustainable alternatives, not eliminating your drive.

The medication adjustment targets both conditions simultaneously. The frameworks give you language and tools to interrupt old patterns in real time. Progress will be gradual and worth tracking carefully. Your capabilities are not in question—the goal is to bring your internal experience into closer alignment with the reality others already see.
