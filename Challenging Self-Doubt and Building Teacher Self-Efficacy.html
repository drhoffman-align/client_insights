Alignment Psychiatry

Rewriting the Narrative

Challenging Self-Doubt and Embracing Evidence of Competence

The Situation

A teacher working with students who have significant emotional and behavioral challenges is managing financial stress, impulsive spending, and episodes of panic linked to recent coping behaviors including gambling and substance use. These patterns appear driven by loneliness and difficulty tolerating emotional discomfort.

Professionally, she carries real responsibilities — building her own curriculum, responding to students' complex needs, and doing so with limited resources. Despite clear evidence of dedication and impact, she consistently discounts her own effectiveness and focuses on perceived failures.

Core Problem

The central issue is an entrenched inability to internalize positive evidence about herself as a teacher. Successes are minimized, shortcomings are magnified, and any acknowledgment of competence triggers discomfort or disbelief. This is not a performance problem — it is a perception problem.

There is a consistent mismatch between how she feels ("I'm not good enough") and what she actually does (what good teachers do). Feelings have been functioning as evidence, when in fact they reflect a belief system — not an accurate read of reality.

Understanding the Dynamics

Negativity Bias

The brain naturally attends to problems over positives. This makes failures feel more real and significant than equivalent evidence of success.

Imposter Syndrome

A pattern where competent people doubt their abilities and fear being exposed as frauds — even when objective evidence says otherwise.

Moving the Goalpost

Shifting standards whenever competence is demonstrated, so acknowledgment of progress is always just out of reach.

Cognitive Restructuring

A technique for identifying and replacing distorted thoughts by grounding self-evaluation in facts rather than feelings.

Framework A: Evidence-Based Self-Assessment

  1. 1List specific actions and qualities that define a good teacher.
  2. 2Compare your own behaviors honestly against that list.
  3. 3Acknowledge wherever your actions meet the criteria — without qualifying.
  4. 4Practice stating positive self-assessments, even if they feel uncomfortable.
  5. 5Repeat regularly to reinforce new beliefs over time.
Applied here: She listed concrete actions — preparing materials, providing snacks, seeking feedback, attending professional development, adapting to students. Each item was connected back to her own definition of a good teacher. Resistance ("it feels like lying") was met by redirecting to the evidence, not the feeling.

Framework B: Try vs. Succeed

  1. 1Recognize that effort is meaningful independent of outcome.
  2. 2Separate trying from the expectation of perfection or immediate results.
  3. 3Give yourself credit for persistence as its own form of competence.
Applied here: She frequently compares herself to idealized others and discounts effort when results aren't perfect. This framework reframes continual effort as a marker of a good teacher, not a consolation prize for falling short.

Practical Instructions

  • Each day, write or say aloud: "I am a good teacher" — belief is not required to begin.
  • Keep a handwritten evidence log of specific actions you took for your students; update it regularly.
  • When self-doubt rises, return to the log — facts, not feelings, should guide your assessment.
  • Notice when you shift standards; pause and return to the original evidence rather than the new bar.

Assignment

Each day until the next session, write down at least one specific action you took that aligns with being a good teacher. Handwrite it — do not type. Practice the affirmation daily. At the next session, bring your log and be ready to discuss what the exercise felt like and whether anything shifted in your thinking.

Key Takeaways

Self-doubt persists despite clear, objective evidence of effectiveness.

Feelings of inadequacy are not reliable indicators of reality.

Effort and persistence are valid markers of competence — not just outcomes.

Comparing yourself to unrealistic standards or others' highlights is distorting.

Saying something before believing it is how belief begins to change.

Discomfort when affirming strengths is normal — it signals a belief being challenged, not a lie being told.

Bottom Line

The gap in this case is not between effort and outcome — it is between reality and perception. The actions are there. The care is there. What is missing is a self-assessment system that counts what is actually happening, rather than defaulting to the most critical interpretation available.

The work ahead is gradual and concrete: document evidence, practice new statements, and allow beliefs to follow behavior over time. This is not about manufacturing confidence — it is about refusing to let feelings override facts.
