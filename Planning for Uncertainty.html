🛡️ Planning for Uncertainty: Building Resilience in Business and Life

Trading hope-based projections for concrete plans when the outcome isn't yours to control

The Situation

The client is currently in India with family following her father-in-law's recent cancer diagnosis and treatment, balancing caregiving, jet lag, and travel demands with running her business remotely.

The business itself is under financial strain — slow sales and an impending rent increase have put pressure on the timeline. She's pursuing a rent reprieve from her landlord while weighing other financial moves, but feels real apprehension about steps like opening a new business bank account or restructuring contracts.

Low energy from jet lag and the sheer weight of family events is compounding all of it.

Core Problem

Planning has leaned on optimistic projections — sales will likely pick up, the landlord will probably grant relief — rather than on confirmed outcomes. That reliance on hope leaves her exposed if either assumption doesn't hold.

Underneath it is a pattern of avoiding the tasks that feel most overwhelming, like restructuring business credit or admin. The apprehension driving that avoidance is at odds with the fact that facing those tasks now, proactively, is what would actually reduce her stress and risk.

Hoped-For vs. Confirmed

Several assumptions currently carrying weight in the business plan haven't actually been confirmed:

Hoped For

  • Sales pick up on their own
  • Landlord grants a reprieve
  • Things resolve before rent is due

Confirmed

  • Current sales trend: slow
  • Landlord's answer: not yet given
  • Rent deadline: fixed, approaching
"Hope doesn't actually equal reality."

Understanding the Dynamics

Procrastination / Avoidance

Delaying a task because it feels overwhelming or anxiety-provoking — which raises both anxiety and actual risk the longer it's put off.

ADHD and Motivation

ADHD can make it hard to act until a deadline is looming — a real driver of last-minute action, but not a substitute for planning ahead of one.

Optimism Bias

The pull toward the best-case outcome as if it were already confirmed — "don't count your chickens before they hatch."

Proactive vs. Reactive Planning

Acting on a risk before it materializes, versus scrambling to respond once it already has.

Framework A

Worst-Case Scenario Planning

1
Identify the risk — e.g., inability to pay rent, continued slow sales.
2
List every outcome, including the worst-case one.
3
Build a concrete plan for each outcome, especially the worst.
4
Identify mitigating resources for each risk.
5
Act proactively, not reactively, once the plan exists.
Applied If the landlord doesn't grant a reprieve, there needs to be a specific plan already in place for managing arrears and communicating with stakeholders — not a plan built after the answer comes back no.

Framework B

Risk-Benefit Analysis for Resource Use

1
List available resources — credit, loans, staff, time, savings.
2
Weigh risk against benefit for each one.
3
Consider both horizons — short-term relief versus long-term cost.
4
Decide from the analysis, not from fear or avoidance.
Applied Opening a new business bank account and pursuing a line of credit have both been avoided out of apprehension. Weighed directly — increased cash flow and the ability to retain key staff against the cost of debt and administrative hassle — the analysis, not the discomfort, should decide it.
1

2

3

4
New account / admin restructuring
5
Contract or staffing changes

Bar length reflects relative complexity and risk — not urgency. Lower-complexity resources are worth confirming first.

Framework C

SMART Goal Setting

1
Make it SMART — Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound.
2
Break it down into actionable steps.
3
Review and adjust on a regular schedule.
Applied Time-blocking and goal-setting are already in use for growth targets. The same structure applies just as well to crisis planning and foundational business tasks — it doesn't need to be reserved for the upside.

Practical Instructions

  • Set a firm calendar date to open the new business bank account — choose it now, not once it's urgent.
  • Ask the landlord directly for a written answer on the reprieve instead of waiting on an assumed yes.
  • Time-block one hour this week solely for the administrative task you've been putting off.
  • Loop in one trusted staff member on the financial picture so it isn't carried alone.
  • Set a checkpoint date to revisit whichever plan turns out not to be needed, so it doesn't quietly go stale.

Assignment

Write out the "what if" scenarios for the business's major risks — no rent reprieve, continued slow sales, inability to cover bills. For each one, note the specific action to take and the resource it draws on.

Send the completed list ahead of the next session.

"It's great to plan for the worst thing possible, and everything else beyond that is better."

Key Takeaways

Optimism about sales or landlord goodwill isn't a plan — it's a placeholder for one.
The task being avoided (new account, credit line) is usually the one carrying the most leverage.
A written worst-case plan removes the need to keep mentally rehearsing it.
Resources look different once compared side by side instead of considered one at a time.
Deadline pressure isn't a planning strategy, even when ADHD makes it an effective one.
Confirmed facts and hoped-for outcomes deserve separate columns, not one blended assumption.
Caregiving abroad and running a business are drawing on the same finite reserve of energy right now.
A plan reviewed on a schedule stays useful; one written and set aside does not.

Bottom Line

The shift underway is from planning around what's hoped for to planning around what's actually confirmed — a distinction that matters more, not less, while she's managing a business from another continent during a family medical crisis.

The tasks she's been avoiding — the new account, the credit line, the direct ask to the landlord — aren't peripheral. They're the leverage points that determine how much room she has if the optimistic version of events doesn't happen. Avoidance here isn't neutral; it's actively narrowing her options.

Resilience, in this context, is specific rather than abstract: a written answer for each worst case, a resource attached to each answer, and a schedule for checking whether any of it still applies. That's what converts uncertainty from a source of dread into something with a plan already attached to it.
