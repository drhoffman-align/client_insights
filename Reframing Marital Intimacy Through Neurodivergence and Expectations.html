Alignment Psychiatry

Rethinking Love and Expectations

Navigating Commitment When Needs Aren't Met

The Situation

There is persistent dissatisfaction in this marriage, particularly around emotional intimacy, affection, and sexual connection. The husband is described as productive and generally a good person, yet consistently falls short in the areas that feel most essential to a fulfilling partnership.

There is a growing awareness that the husband may have traits associated with asexuality, aromanticism, or neurodivergence — which complicates how his behaviors are interpreted and what is realistic to expect going forward.

Core Problem

The central tension is a mismatch between deeply held expectations of marital intimacy and the husband's consistent inability — or limited capacity — to meet them. This gap produces chronic frustration and emotional exhaustion.

Compounding this is a pattern of delaying difficult decisions until pain becomes unbearable, and a noticeable gap between the advice given to others in similar situations and what is being practiced here. Hoping for gradual change in the husband continues despite longstanding evidence that change is unlikely.

Understanding the Dynamics

Asexuality

A sexual orientation characterized by little or no sexual attraction. His lack of sexual initiative may reflect his orientation, not a judgment of his partner.

Aromanticism

Limited or absent romantic attraction. Minimal displays of affection may stem from this orientation rather than indifference or neglect.

Neurodivergence / Autism

Differences in how love and connection are expressed. Care may be present but communicated in ways that are difficult to recognize.

Dropping Expectations

Releasing hopes that are repeatedly unmet. Not "settling" — a deliberate choice to reduce suffering when change is unlikely.

Framework 1 — Incremental Decision-Making

  1. 1Recognize that big decisions do not need to be made quickly.
  2. 2Take small, manageable steps rather than trying to resolve everything at once.
  3. 3Prioritize reflection and processing before taking any action.
  4. 4Set one small, achievable goal around a difficult decision each week.
How this applies: The tendency here is to avoid hard choices until situations become intolerable. This framework allows for starting small — voicing a need, setting a boundary — rather than feeling pressure to make an all-or-nothing decision.

Framework 2 — Expectations vs. Reality Reframing

  1. 1Identify the core expectations held for this relationship.
  2. 2Examine whether those expectations are realistic given the husband's actual capacity.
  3. 3Explore where these expectations originated — commitment, upbringing, societal norms.
  4. 4Consider what it costs emotionally to hold onto expectations that consistently go unmet.
How this applies: Understanding the gap between what is wanted and what is possible reduces self-blame and supports either informed acceptance or informed change — both are valid paths.

Practical Instructions

  • Notice how the husband expresses care this week, viewed through the lens of capacity rather than intent.
  • Observe emotional reactions when needs are or aren't met — without judgment, just awareness.
  • Avoid making large decisions; stay focused on small, deliberate steps.
  • Journal reflections on whether understanding his limitations shifts how his actions feel.

Assignment

Throughout the week, sit with this question: If your husband has a condition — asexuality, aromanticism, or autism — that genuinely limits his capacity for intimacy, would that change how you interpret his actions? When moments arise where needs go unmet, pause and ask: "If this is about his capacity rather than his choice, how do I feel?" Write down what surfaces. Bring those reflections to the next session.

Key Takeaways

Big decisions can be approached gradually — urgency is not required.
Small, thoughtful steps are more sustainable than waiting for a crisis.
His limited intimacy may reflect capacity, not intent or lack of care.
Expectations rooted in commitment and societal norms deserve examination.
Releasing unmet expectations is a tool for reducing pain, not weakness.
Reflection — not immediate action — is the healthiest next step right now.

Bottom Line

This session focused on slowing down the decision-making process and examining the true source of marital dissatisfaction. The possibility that the husband's limitations stem from neurodivergence or asexuality offers a compassionate reframe — one that reduces personal rejection and opens space for a more accurate understanding of what he can and cannot offer.

The work for now is not to decide anything. It is to observe, reflect, and consider whether a new lens changes the emotional experience. Feelings and needs are valid. Both acceptance and change remain open paths. Clarity comes from understanding, not from urgency.
