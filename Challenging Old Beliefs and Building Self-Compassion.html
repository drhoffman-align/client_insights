Rewriting Self-Belief

Challenging Old Narratives and Building New Truths

The Situation

Persistent feelings of inadequacy and self-criticism are showing up most sharply in your professional life as a teacher. Even considering positive statements about yourself — such as "I care about my students' learning" — feels emotionally painful rather than affirming.

This discomfort is rooted in early family messages that labeled you as selfish, lazy, or inconsiderate. Combined with the real challenges of ADHD and anxiety, those messages became internalized beliefs that consistently override positive feedback from others — even when that feedback is credible and accurate.

Core Problem

The central issue is a deeply held belief that you don't deserve positive recognition. This belief wasn't formed by current evidence — it was shaped by old family narratives that went unchallenged for a long time. Because all incoming information gets filtered through that belief, positive feedback feels false or even threatening rather than true.

There is a clear gap between how others consistently perceive you (kind, caring, giving) and how you experience yourself (undeserving, inadequate). Closing that gap is the work.

Understanding the Dynamics

Beliefs as "Unverified Truths"Beliefs feel factual because they've been reinforced over time — but they are not the same as facts. They can be updated with new evidence.
Belief FilteringYour existing beliefs act as a lens, causing you to notice and retain negative information while discounting positive feedback as implausible.
ADHD & MisattributionADHD-related behaviors (disorganization, impulsivity) are often mislabeled as laziness or selfishness. They are symptoms, not character flaws.
Learned DiscomfortThe pain you feel when encountering positive self-statements is a conditioned response to old messages — not evidence that the positive statement is wrong.

Frameworks

1 — CBT Belief-Perception-Action Cycle

1
Recognize that you carry a "bubble" of beliefs that shapes how you interpret everything.
2
Identify the specific belief filtering your experience (e.g., "I don't deserve positive things").
3
Challenge the belief by asking: is this a fact, or an old, unverified story?
4
Actively gather concrete evidence that contradicts the old belief.
5
Repeatedly expose yourself to the new belief, even when it feels uncomfortable.
6
Re-evaluate as evidence accumulates — the new belief gradually becomes more credible.
How This AppliesWhen you see the posted statement and feel discomfort, that's step 5 in action. The discomfort signals that old and new beliefs are in conflict — not that the new belief is wrong. Pausing to find one supporting example is steps 4 and 6 happening in real time.

2 — Evidence Collection as Belief Revision

1
Treat positive self-statements as hypotheses to be tested, not claims to be accepted or rejected.
2
Record one small piece of supporting evidence each time you encounter the statement.
3
Review the accumulated list periodically to observe the pattern over time.
How This AppliesOld beliefs were built by repeated negative messages over years. New beliefs are built the same way — through repetition. Each written example is one data point added to a growing counter-narrative.

Practical Instructions

  • Post "I care about my students' learning" somewhere you will see it every day at work or home.
  • Each time you see it and feel discomfort, pause — don't dismiss or argue against the statement.
  • Identify one concrete example that supports the statement (e.g., staying late, preparing extra materials).
  • Write the example down, even if it feels minor — the act of recording it matters more than its size.

Assignment

Post the statement "I care about my students' learning" where you will see it daily. Each time you notice it — especially when you feel discomfort — write down one specific reason or example that supports it. Do this every day until the next session, then bring your list. The goal is not to feel convinced immediately; it is to begin building a record of evidence your mind can work with over time.

Key Takeaways

Beliefs ≠ FactsThey feel true because they've been repeated — but they can be revised with new evidence.
Discomfort Is ExpectedPain when practicing new beliefs is part of the process, not a sign that something is wrong.
ADHD Is Not a Character FlawBehaviors driven by ADHD are symptoms, not evidence of laziness or selfishness.
Old Narratives Are Not the Only TruthFamily criticism shaped your self-concept but does not define your actual worth or behavior.
Outside Feedback Has WeightColleagues and students who see you as caring are offering credible data — don't default to discounting it.
Change Is GradualRepeated small exposures to new evidence — not a single insight — are what shift beliefs over time.

Bottom Line

Your discomfort with positive self-statements is not proof that those statements are false. It is the predictable result of old beliefs, formed early and reinforced often, colliding with new information. The pain is real — and it is also a learned response, not an accurate measure of your worth or your impact as a teacher.

The path forward is consistent, repeated exposure to evidence that challenges the old narrative. This does not require feeling convinced right away. It requires showing up to the exercise — posting the statement, noticing the discomfort, and writing down one example — until the new belief has enough of its own evidence to stand on.
