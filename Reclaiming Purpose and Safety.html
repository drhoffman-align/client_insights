✍️ Reclaiming Purpose and Safety: Navigating Return to Work and Restructuring Personal Narratives

Rewriting a story toward accuracy is not self-deception — it's correcting a distortion

The Situation

Anxiety and discomfort have followed a return to a teaching and leadership role after a leave of absence. Colleagues' comments land as judgment about the time away, triggering inadequacy and self-doubt. The leave itself was taken to escape overwhelming stress — school, marriage difficulties, and dual job roles — and was genuinely restorative, but disappointment lingers over not having secured a new job during it, alongside a real loss of purpose in the current role.

Alongside this sits the emotional aftermath of a divorce, including persistent negative narratives about the relationship and self-worth that keep resurfacing.

Core Problem

Accepting the need for leave, and the current circumstances that followed it, is difficult under a sense of judgment from others and internalized expectations. Feeling stuck between a job that no longer aligns with genuine passion and an aspiration to become a licensed counselor compounds the strain.

There's a mismatch between feelings of failure and the reality that these choices were necessary for well-being. Persistent negative narratives about the marriage and divorce continue to erode self-esteem and a sense of agency.

"You took leave because you needed restoration, not because you failed."

Understanding the Dynamics

Judgment Perception vs. Reality

Perceived judgment from colleagues doesn't always match what was actually said or meant — the gap is worth examining before absorbing it as fact.

Non-Linear Progress

Professional transitions rarely move in a straight line, even when real, meaningful progress is happening underneath the surface.

Self-Worth Independent of External Validation

A sense of worth anchored to a job title or relationship status will rise and fall with things outside personal control.

Vocational Disillusionment

The specific pain of losing connection to a role that used to feel meaningful — distinct from not yet knowing what comes next.

Framework A

Narrative Restructuring

1
Identify the current narrative — "I failed because I took leave," "My spouse left because I wasn't enough."
2
Gather objective facts — what actually happened, what choices were made, and why.
3
Reframe from a self-compassionate, empowered perspective.
4
Practice repeating and integrating the new narrative until it holds.
Applied Persistent thoughts about the divorce — "he left me first," "I wasn't enough" — get reframed to acknowledge real effort and the reality of a mutual decision: the ex-partner chose to separate first, and staying meant protecting personal well-being.
Old narrative "I failed because I took leave."
→
New narrative "I took leave to restore myself."

Framework B

Purpose and Goal Clarification

1
Define the life you want — clarity, autonomy, balance.
2
Identify what's needed to achieve that life — credentials, job changes, boundaries.
3
Bridge the gap by mapping actionable steps from current reality to desired future.
Applied Dissatisfaction with teaching and leadership gets reframed by seeing the current job as a stepping stone toward a counseling license — a strategic move for stability while the actual passion is being worked toward, not a dead end.

Bridge, Not a Prison

Prison framing

  • Trapped, no visible way out
  • Time here feels like defeat
  • No connection to the future

Bridge framing

  • Temporary, purposeful, leads somewhere
  • Time here builds toward the goal
  • Directly connected to counseling ahead

Framework C

Boundary-Setting and Self-Protection at Work

1
Identify what boundaries are needed for safety and well-being.
2
Understand legal and contractual protections already in place.
3
Enforce boundaries — adhere to job requirements, document professionalism, refuse unreasonable demands.
4
Prepare for pushback by maintaining records and professionalism.
Applied Contract and legal protections exist to prevent overextension. Tasks outside the actual job description aren't an obligation, and documenting professionalism is what protects against disputes later.
"Rewriting your story is an act of self-respect."

Practical Instructions

  • When a colleague's comment triggers a spiral of self-judgment, write down what was actually said versus what you assumed it meant.
  • Before responding to an unreasonable request at work, check your job description first, then respond from what it actually says.
  • Pick one weekly ritual that reconnects you to why you wanted to become a counselor in the first place, even a small one.
  • When an old narrative about the divorce resurfaces, name it as "old story" out loud before deciding whether to engage with it.
  • At the end of each week, note one boundary you held, separate from any moment you didn't.

Assignment

Set aside 30-60 minutes to write your ideal life vision, then list what you'd actually need to get there — credentials, job changes, boundaries — and outline concrete steps you could take now. Bring your written vision and steps to the next session.

Key Takeaways

Taking leave for restoration and taking leave because of failure are two different stories about the same decision.
A job that no longer fits doesn't have to be abandoned immediately to still function as a stepping stone.
Perceived judgment from colleagues doesn't always match what was actually said or meant.
A divorce narrative that assigns all the fault to yourself is rarely the complete or accurate version of events.
A contract defines the actual scope of what's owed at work — anything beyond it is a choice, not an obligation.
Professional transitions rarely move in a straight line, even when real progress is happening underneath.
Self-worth tied to a job title or relationship status will rise and fall with things outside your control.
Writing down a vision for the future is what turns a vague sense of stuckness into an actual set of steps.

Bottom Line

Returning to a job that no longer fits isn't a defeat. It's a strategic choice made from the options actually available at the time, and treating it as failure only adds unnecessary weight to a decision that was already the right one.

A story that places all responsibility for a divorce on one person rarely reflects what actually happened. Rewriting it toward accuracy isn't self-deception — it's correcting a distortion that's been doing real damage to self-worth.

Narrative and boundaries are both, in the end, about accurately locating responsibility — for what happened in the marriage, and for what's actually owed at work — so energy stops going toward guilt that was never earned and starts going toward the counseling career still being built.
