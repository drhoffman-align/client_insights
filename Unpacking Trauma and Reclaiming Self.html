💼 Unpacking Trauma and Reclaiming Self: Moving from Survival to Healing

Learning that strength can include being seen, not only holding everything together

The Situation

Severe sleep issues, anxiety, and depression have worsened over the past five years under the weight of ongoing trauma. Professionally, that included racism, sexism, and workplace betrayal, culminating in unjust termination from a high-level executive role. Personally, it included surviving a dangerous relationship with real threats to her safety and ongoing fear.

The symptoms are wide-reaching: a disorganized living space, sporadic eating and significant weight gain, racing thoughts, physical anxiety with a racing heartbeat, emotional numbness, and periods of collapse. Current medications — escitalopram, hydroxyzine, and amlodipine — don't feel like they're fully addressing what's happening. A sleep study is pending, and blood pressure is being monitored at home.

Isolation, feeling misunderstood, and being overwhelmed by everything being carried are all present, alongside real difficulty accessing vulnerability or self-compassion.

Core Problem

Years of unprocessed trauma and chronic stress have accumulated into PTSD, major depressive disorder, and anxiety. The coping strategy has been to "stay strong" and keep moving — masking pain and vulnerability — and that strategy has now reached its limit.

There's a real mismatch between how things look from outside (functioning, strong) and what's actually happening internally (overwhelmed, exhausted, fearful, emotionally shut down). Grief, betrayal, and loss have never had the space or support needed to actually be processed, and that absence is now showing up in both emotional and physical symptoms.

"Your body is actually keeping a score of all the stuff you've been through, and it is actually showing up."

What's Shown vs. What's Underneath

What's shown

  • Bandaged, made to look decent
  • "Nobody knows"
  • Still functioning, still showing up

What's underneath

  • Wounds still open, untended
  • Grief never fully processed
  • Genuine exhaustion and collapse

Understanding the Dynamics

PTSD

A condition where the mind and body remain in a state of alarm after trauma — showing up here as fear, racing thoughts, and a sense of being hunted.

Major Depressive Disorder

Persistent low mood, loss of interest, and disrupted sleep, appetite, and functioning — reflected in the disorganized home, weight change, and emotional collapse.

Anxiety Disorder

Excessive worry paired with physical symptoms — racing thoughts and a racing heartbeat are both present here, mental and physical anxiety together.

Obstructive Sleep Apnea

A disorder where breathing repeatedly stops and starts during sleep, often linked to weight and stress — the pending sleep study will help clarify its role here.

Medication Effects

An antidepressant is meant to "raise the floor" of mood, preventing the deepest lows — it's currently unclear whether that's actually happening.

Framework A

The Baggage and Bones Model

1
Recognize what's being carried — trauma, pain, unresolved experience.
2
Acknowledge what "being strong" has meant — hiding wounds, not healing them.
3
Understand capacity — the pain spilling out now is a signal for change, not a failure.
4
Begin unpacking with support — sorting through, rather than just covering up.
Applied The "organized chaos" at home and the emotional collapse are both signs of carrying too much for too long. A coping style built on masking and pushing through has done real work up to this point — and it isn't sustainable as the only strategy going forward.

Framework B

Stages of Grief

1
Identify the stages — denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance.
2
Notice what's been skipped — especially depression and acceptance.
3
Allow each stage to be felt, even when it's uncomfortable.
4
Move toward acceptance — where real understanding and peace can begin.
Applied The full depth of what's been lost — professionally, personally, and in a broader sense — hasn't yet been allowed to be felt. Going through depression, rather than around it, is a necessary part of this process, not a sign that something has gone wrong.

Framework C

The "Feeling Alive" Principle

1
Find what makes you feel present — being near water, riding a motorcycle, whatever it is.
2
Use it as an anchor during the harder parts of this process.
3
Balance it with the real work — not a substitute for emotional processing, but a counterweight to it.
Applied Time by the water is already a lifeline. Seeking it out intentionally, as part of the healing process rather than only as an escape from it, is what turns it into something sustainable.
"You have to really learn how to be strong for yourself, which means you have to be vulnerable and take care of yourself."

Practical Instructions

  • Before tidying the "organized chaos" at home, pause and notice what feeling shows up when you look at it.
  • When you catch yourself "putting makeup on" a hard feeling before seeing anyone, name the actual feeling first, even silently.
  • Log blood pressure and sleep at the same time each day, in the same place, so patterns are easier to see later.
  • Choose one trusted person to share a single small piece of what you're carrying this week.
  • When exhaustion hits, rest before pushing through it, and notice what's different when you do.

Assignment

Begin a "Baggage Inventory" journal. Each day, write down one thing you're carrying — an emotion, a memory, a fear, a pain — how it affects you, and whether you've ever shared it with anyone. Ten to fifteen minutes daily, written honestly and without editing. Start today, and continue until the next session; bring it along if it feels comfortable to share.

Key Takeaways

A home environment and a body can both reach capacity for what's being carried, showing signs of collapse well before the mind acknowledges it.
Racism, workplace betrayal, and relationship danger are three separate traumas, not one generalized hard stretch.
A stable-looking medication regimen can still not be adequately treating what's actually present.
Grief that's never fully entered doesn't disappear — it waits, and holding it off takes real, ongoing energy.
A sleep study result will help clarify whether physiology, trauma, or both are driving the exhaustion.
Moments that create a felt sense of being alive aren't a distraction from the healing work — they're part of what makes it sustainable.
Weight change, disorganization, and emotional numbness are documented features of major depression, not evidence of personal failure.
Masking pain well enough that no one else notices has come at real cost to her own recovery.

Bottom Line

Years of racism, betrayal, an unjust termination, and a genuinely dangerous relationship didn't resolve simply because functioning continued through all of it. The body and the home environment are now showing what the mind had been asked to override for a long time.

The version of strength that made it through all of that required hiding the wound, and that version has reached its limit. What's needed now is a different kind of strength — one that includes being seen while still in the process of healing, not only after it's finished.

Medication review, an actual answer from the sleep study, and grief that was never fully entered are all part of the same plan, not separate problems competing for attention. None of it requires recovery to already be underway before the work can start.
