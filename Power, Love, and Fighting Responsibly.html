📊 Letting Go of "Should": Power, Love, and Fighting Responsibly

A role in your life and the actions behind it are two separate things worth checking against each other

The Situation

Repeated experiences of feeling harmed or let down by people close by — family, partners, colleagues — have taken a real toll. A persistent gap runs between what feels owed, in terms of love and respect, and what others actually provide. Multiple chances tend to get extended, hoping for reciprocity, but the result is usually feeling drained and extracted from instead.

At work, covert power struggles — silent treatment, indirect sabotage — leave a real sense of powerlessness and frustration.

Core Problem

A tendency to overvalue others' "brand" or reputation for love — giving them power based on hope and belief rather than evidence of their actual capacity — leads to repeated disappointment, emotional extraction, and real difficulty setting boundaries.

There's a mismatch between feelings — hope, belief in someone's potential — and the reality of their behavior, which may reflect limited capacity, blind spots, or even intentional harm.

"Brand times value equals power."

Understanding the Dynamics

Extraction

When someone repeatedly takes — emotionally, energetically — without giving anything back in return.

Blind Spot

A behavior someone is unaware of that still causes harm. Not always intentional, but still real in its impact.

Coping Mechanism

A learned behavior used to protect oneself, sometimes at real expense to the people around them.

Silent Treatment

Withholding communication or engagement as a covert form of power struggle, rather than a neutral pause.

Framework A

Love as Brand × Value = Power

BrandThe image or role someone has — "family," "partner," "boss"
×
ValueTheir actual worth or capacity, based on evidence
=
PowerTheir influence over you
Applied Power has often been given based on role and hope — "they're my parent, so they must love me" — rather than demonstrated value. Continued investment in relationships where actions didn't match words has led to repeated harm.

Framework B

Fighting Responsibly

1
Direct confrontation — address issues openly, with evidence, and clarifying questions.
2
Let people prove your case — state observations in writing and track patterns over time.
3
Avoid indirect power struggles — covert tactics like the silent treatment stay unaddressed; bring issues into the open instead.
Applied Covert power struggles at work — like the silent treatment — get met with directness and documentation instead of matching the covert approach, which shifts the power dynamic rather than absorbing it.

Pleading and Hoping vs. Assessing and Acting

Before Pleads, yells, or hopes others will eventually change
→
After Assesses value realistically, sets boundaries, acts on evidence

Framework C

Acceptance and Letting Go of "Should"

1
Recognize the difference between hope or expectation and reality.
2
Accept — let go of what you wish people would do, and focus on what they actually do.
3
Protect yourself by using that acceptance to set boundaries and prioritize your own well-being.
Applied "Should" thinking — the hope, the reputation, the belief — keeps investment going in places evidence doesn't support. Reality, not hope, is what boundaries actually get built on.
"When someone shows you who they are, believe them."

Practical Instructions

  • Before extending another chance to someone who's disappointed you, write down what evidence, not hope, would need to change for that decision to make sense.
  • When someone goes quiet instead of addressing an issue directly, name the silence out loud rather than filling it with your own guesses.
  • Keep a running note titled "role" and a separate one titled "actions" for anyone central in your life, and check whether the two lists actually match.
  • After stating your case once, clearly, resist the urge to repeat or escalate it — let the next response speak for itself.
  • When you catch yourself thinking "they should," rewrite the sentence starting with "they have" instead, based only on what's already happened.

Assignment

Review your notes from this and last week's session, especially on acceptance. Reflect on where you're holding onto "should" in your relationships, and jot down any insights or questions. Come ready to discuss examples where you noticed "should" thinking and how you responded.

Key Takeaways

A person's role in your life and their demonstrated behavior are two separate things worth evaluating independently.
Repeated harm, once documented, becomes a pattern rather than an isolated incident open to reinterpretation.
A blind spot and a deliberate choice can produce identical harm, but they call for different responses once named.
Stating your case once and observing what follows uses far less energy than repeating yourself indefinitely.
Silent treatment is a form of power play, not a neutral absence of conflict.
Self-love shows up as protective action, not just as a feeling held privately.
Letting go of "should" doesn't mean giving up hope — it means basing decisions on what's already happened.
Directness at work, backed by documentation, shifts leverage away from covert tactics.

Bottom Line

Giving someone power based on their role — parent, partner, boss — rather than their actual, demonstrated capacity is what turns hope into a liability. That's a decision that can be made differently once the gap between the two is actually visible.

Repeated disappointment from people close by isn't a sign of being too sensitive or too demanding. It's the predictable result of investing based on brand while ignoring evidence of value — a pattern that can be interrupted going forward.

Acceptance here isn't surrender, and it isn't lowering the bar. It's redirecting energy that's been spent hoping people will become who they haven't shown themselves to be, toward decisions and boundaries grounded in who they actually are.
