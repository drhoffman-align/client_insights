Finding Agency Amidst Anxiety

Reframing Control and Emotional Expression

The Situation

You are balancing a demanding role as a teacher, multiple school responsibilities, and the transition into new motherhood. Social connection is your primary source of joy, and contentment — not happiness — is what you're working toward. Anxiety, low mood, and occasional overwhelm cycle through your daily experience.

At work, you feel obligated to take on additional duties, particularly those tied to your identity, and struggle to decline. Recent conflicts at home and difficult moments with students have compounded the pressure, leaving you emotionally stretched and unsure of your options.

Core Problem

The central issue is a belief that you lack agency — that you must comply with what others expect, especially at work. This belief drives chronic anxiety and emotional suppression, as expressing frustration or declining tasks feels risky or "rude."

When feelings go unexpressed to those who trigger them, stress accumulates internally. Venting to trusted people provides temporary relief but doesn't resolve the underlying dynamic or create actual change.

Understanding the Dynamics

AnxietyA feeling rooted in perceived threat or uncertainty — it can motivate action or become paralyzing depending on its intensity.
AgencyYour capacity to make choices and act on them. You have more of it than you currently recognize, including at work.
Emotional SuppressionHolding feelings in to avoid conflict or judgment. Over time, this increases anxiety, resentment, and a sense of powerlessness.
DefensivenessA signal that a boundary may be threatened. Rather than reacting, it can be used as a cue to clarify your needs.

Frameworks

1 — Anxiety as a Spectrum

  • 1Recognize anxiety as a feeling, not a flaw or character weakness.
  • 2Identify what's driving it: worry, safety, security, or expectations.
  • 3Distinguish helpful anxiety (motivates action) from harmful anxiety (disrupts functioning).
  • 4Locate yourself on the curve — some anxiety is useful; too much becomes harmful.
How This AppliesYour anxiety isn't always the problem — sometimes it signals that a boundary is being crossed or action is needed. The goal is to assess its function, not eliminate it.

2 — Agency and Direct Expression

  • 1Question the belief that you "have to" comply — most situations involve a choice.
  • 2Assess whether the task or situation crosses a boundary or causes harm.
  • 3Notice when you're venting to safe people rather than speaking to those involved.
  • 4Practice stating your need directly and professionally, even in small ways.
  • 5Reflect on what happened and adjust your approach as needed.
How This AppliesTalking about a problem with a friend doesn't create safety with the person causing it. Direct, respectful communication is what actually shifts the dynamic.

Practical Instructions

  • When anxiety spikes, pause and ask: is this motivating me or overwhelming me?
  • Before accepting an extra task, ask yourself: "Do I actually have a choice here?"
  • Practice one direct, calm statement of need — "I'm not able to take this on right now" counts.
  • When you notice defensiveness, treat it as information: something may need to be said or changed.

Assignment

This week, identify one situation where you feel anxious or obligated. Pause to assess whether you have a choice, then practice stating a need or limit — however small — directly to the person involved. Reflect briefly on how it felt and what changed. Bring one example to the next session.

Key Takeaways

Anxiety is a normal feeling that can sometimes signal useful information.
When anxiety disrupts daily functioning, it's crossed into harmful territory.
You have more choices than you typically recognize, especially at work.
Complying by default is a choice — so is setting a limit.
Venting to others doesn't resolve issues with those who are actually involved.
Honest, respectful communication is both possible and necessary for your well-being.

Bottom Line

This session focused on two core shifts: seeing anxiety as a signal rather than a threat, and recognizing that you have genuine agency in situations that feel mandatory. The pattern of suppressing emotions to avoid conflict is understandable, but it keeps the problem in place and compounds your stress over time.

The path forward is not about eliminating anxiety or forcing happiness — it's about pausing, assessing your choices, and communicating your needs directly and professionally. Small steps in that direction are enough to begin building a greater sense of control and contentment.
