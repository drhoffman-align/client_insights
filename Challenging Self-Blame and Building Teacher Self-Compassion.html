Rewriting the Narrative:
Challenging Self-Blame & Building Self-Compassion

Therapeutic Insights Document — For Personal Use in Ongoing Treatment

The Situation

Teaching is a high-demand profession under ordinary circumstances. When someone brings a chronic baseline of anxiety into that environment — one shaped by early experiences of not belonging and feeling that their emotions don't matter — the daily pressures of the classroom become significantly harder to carry. Challenging incidents, unsupportive leadership, and unpredictable student behavior all land with greater weight than they might for someone starting from a calmer internal state.

Over time, a pattern has developed in which difficult outcomes at work are absorbed personally, even when the evidence points elsewhere. Administrative decisions, student behavior, and environmental factors are filtered through a lens that assigns responsibility inward. This isn't a character flaw — it's a learned response, one that made a certain kind of sense given earlier life experiences where self-monitoring felt necessary for safety.

What makes this particularly complex is the contradiction at its center: the same person who doubts her adequacy is recognized by colleagues and students for genuine dedication, care, and advocacy. The feelings of inadequacy are real and persistent — but they do not accurately reflect the actual record of what has been done and who has been protected.

Core Problem

The central issue is a deeply embedded belief that functions almost like a rule: when something goes wrong, it is my fault. This belief operates automatically — before evidence is considered, before context is weighed, before anyone else's role is acknowledged. It creates a closed loop where any negative outcome confirms the belief, and positive outcomes are either dismissed or attributed to luck rather than skill.
