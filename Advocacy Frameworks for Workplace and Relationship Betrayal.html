Alignment Psychiatry

Standing Up in the Face of Retaliation and Relationship Betrayal

Therapeutic Insights Document

The Situation

You are navigating serious workplace adversity — retaliation from supervisors, denial of accommodations and resources, and procedural violations that have undermined your ability to do your job. You have documented evidence of these issues, including canceled programs, withheld tools, and exclusion from key communications.

At the same time, you are processing the emotional fallout from a relationship in which your partner was unfaithful, blamed you for the consequences, and withdrew support during an already difficult period. Together, these experiences have produced feelings of frustration, confusion, abandonment, and overwhelm.

Core Problem

The central issue is a pattern of systemic invalidation — in both your workplace and your intimate relationship, your legitimate needs and boundaries have been reframed as problems, failings, or insubordination. Others' narratives have been imposed over the reality of your actions and intentions.

This creates a painful gap between who you are and how you are being characterized, and that gap breeds self-doubt. The work here is to close that gap by grounding yourself in facts and the full truth of what actually occurred.

Understanding the Dynamics

RetaliationWhen an employer takes adverse action because an employee asserted their rights. Program cancellations, resource denial, and insubordination accusations following accommodation requests are examples.
Procedural ViolationFailure to follow established workplace policies — such as withholding access, skipping required timelines, or blocking essential communications.
InsubordinationRefusing a lawful, reasonable directive. Including HR or union representatives in emails when you feel unsafe does not meet this definition — it is a protected act.
Self-BlameThe tendency to internalize responsibility for outcomes caused by others. Telling yourself the full, accurate story is the direct antidote to this pattern.

Frameworks

1 — Fact-Based Advocacy

1
Gather and organize all evidence (emails, photos, documentation) by date and violation type.
2
Present events in sequential order — what happened, not why or who is to blame.
3
Open and close your presentation by stating your qualifications and goals.
4
Reference specific documents as needed ("see exhibit X") and let the evidence lead.
5
Allow HR, union, or investigators to draw their own conclusions from the facts.
Applied here: You included HR and union reps on emails only in situations involving employment protections. Presenting this fact chronologically, without assigning motive, demonstrates the pattern of retaliation clearly and professionally.

2 — Full Story Self-Talk

1
When processing pain, tell yourself the complete story — not only the parts that induce guilt.
2
Identify what actually happened, who made which choices, and how you responded.
3
Separate your actions from others' misconduct — you are not responsible for their choices.
4
Use the full narrative to validate your feelings and reinforce your actual innocence.
Applied here: Your partner was unfaithful, was confronted, and then shifted blame onto you before withdrawing. That is the full sequence. The abandonment reflects his response to accountability — not your worth or failure.

Practical Instructions

  • Organize all workplace evidence chronologically — emails, photos, documentation — grouped by violation type.
  • Draft a factual narrative focused on what happened; omit speculation about intent or blame.
  • Use neutral, policy-referenced language in all official communications with HR, union, or investigators.
  • Practice the Full Story exercise whenever self-blame surfaces — write it out if needed to see the sequence clearly.

Assignment

Before your next session or union meeting, draft a written summary of workplace events using the Fact-Based Advocacy Framework. Begin with your qualifications and goals, present events chronologically without assigning blame, reference specific documentation, and close by restating your goals. Bring the draft and your organized evidence to review together.

Key Takeaways

Present your case through facts and organized evidence, not blame.
Open and close all formal communications with your goals and qualifications.
Including HR or union reps in emails for protection is lawful — not insubordination.
Procedural violations and retaliation are not your fault or your failure.
Tell yourself the full story — your innocence is part of the narrative too.
Your partner's blame-shifting and withdrawal reflect his choices, not your value.

Bottom Line

You are dealing with two simultaneous betrayals — one professional, one personal — both of which have involved others reframing your reasonable actions as the problem. The frameworks from this session give you concrete tools to counter that pattern: present the facts clearly at work, and tell yourself the honest, complete story in your personal processing.

Your next steps are to organize your evidence, draft your factual narrative, and continue practicing self-validation. The distress you feel is a proportionate response to real events. Your actions have been protective and justified, and you have the grounding to advocate for yourself effectively.
