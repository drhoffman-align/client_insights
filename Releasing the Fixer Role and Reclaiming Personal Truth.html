Letting Go of the Fixer Role

Honoring Your Truth Without Carrying Others' Burdens

The Situation

Old acquaintances have resurfaced, bringing misrepresentation and social media accusations that reactivate past relational wounds. At work, warnings go unheeded, reinforcing a persistent feeling of not being heard or believed.

Beneath both situations is a lifelong pattern: absorbing others' emotions, feeling responsible for resolving conflict, and internalizing blame — a pattern rooted in early experiences of being cast as "the problem."

Core Problem

The central issue is an ingrained belief that others' distress or distorted perceptions are yours to fix. When that fixing fails, the result is frustration, resentment, and a compounding sense of injustice.

There is a clear mismatch between what you know logically ("I'm not the problem") and what you feel emotionally ("I must be, or they wouldn't react this way"). That gap is where over-functioning and exhaustion take root.

Understanding the Dynamics

Distortion

When someone's perception is shaped by their own trauma or bias, causing them to misplace blame onto others.

Emotional Contagion

Absorbing others' emotional states and feeling compelled to resolve discomfort that isn't yours to carry.

The Fixer Role

A compulsion to solve others' problems, typically rooted in childhood dynamics where your worth was tied to managing conflict.

Acceptance

Allowing others to hold their own reality without taking responsibility for correcting or softening it.

Framework: The Distortion / Truth Process

  • 1Notice the distortion — recognize when blame or a false narrative is being projected onto you.
  • 2Check logic vs. belief — separate what you know from what you emotionally feel in the moment.
  • 3State your truth once — calmly and clearly, without over-explaining or arguing.
  • 4Return the illusion — give the distortion back; don't absorb it as your problem to fix.
  • 5Let go of the outcome — others may not accept your truth, and that's not yours to control.
  • 6Withdraw excess energy — redirect focus to your own needs and goals.
How This Applies With the social media situation: acknowledge the distortion, state your truth if needed, then disengage entirely — those people no longer affect your real life. At work: present your evidence clearly, then step back. If leadership ignores it, that consequence belongs to them, not you.

Practical Instructions

  • When you feel blamed, pause first: ask yourself whether this is genuinely about you.
  • State your position once — clearly, calmly, and without elaboration — then stop.
  • At work, present evidence and escalate if warranted; don't keep relitigating ignored warnings.
  • With old acquaintances, it's acceptable to say you'd prefer not to receive updates about irrelevant drama.

Assignment

This week, practice the State Your Truth Once rule in any situation where you feel blamed or misunderstood. Say it once, then do not argue or over-explain. Afterward, journal briefly: What did you feel? Was it hard to stop? Did you notice relief, frustration, or something else? Bring those reflections to the next session.

Key Takeaways

You are not responsible for fixing others' distorted perceptions.

State your truth once — over-explaining rarely changes anything.

People operate from their own histories; correction is usually not possible.

Absorbing blame that isn't yours deepens old wounds, not resolution.

Letting go of the outcome is how resentment stops compounding.

Energy spent on frustration is energy unavailable for your own growth.

Bottom Line

This session focused on stepping out of the fixer role — honoring your truth, stating it clearly, and then releasing the need for it to be accepted. Others' perceptions of you are not your responsibility to manage or correct.

Acceptance here doesn't mean agreement — it means recognizing what is and isn't yours to carry. Withdrawing from others' distortions is not defeat; it is how you protect your mental health and reclaim your energy for things that are actually yours.
