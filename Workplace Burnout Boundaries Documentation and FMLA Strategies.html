Breaking the Cycle: Boundaries, Burnout, and Reclaiming Control at Work

A therapeutic insights document prepared following your session

The Situation

You are navigating a workplace that consistently makes poor decisions without input from technical staff, then expects you to absorb the fallout. The result has been escalating stress — anxiety attacks, insomnia, withdrawal from activities that restore you, and a steady erosion of your sense of control.

This has been building over your year at this company, compounded by a longer history of over-functioning in professional settings. The physical and emotional symptoms you're experiencing are not overreactions; they are predictable consequences of an unsustainable situation.

Core Problem

The central issue is not your performance — it is that you have been internalizing systemic failures as personal shortcomings. When leadership creates chaos, you step in to fix it, which temporarily masks the problem but ultimately leads to missed deliverables, shame, and deeper burnout.

You understand intellectually that these failures are not yours to own, yet the emotional response — self-blame, shame, overwork — continues. The work ahead involves aligning how you feel with what you already know to be true.

Understanding the Dynamics

Over-Functioning

Taking on responsibilities beyond your role to compensate for others' failures — unsustainable and a direct path to burnout.

Misplaced Blame

When systemic failures land on the person left holding the bag, not the decision-makers who created them.

Shame

A self-directed emotion triggered by perceived failure — here, activated by situations that are not actually your fault.

Boundaries

Clear limits around your time and responsibilities that protect your well-being and keep your role defined.

Framework 1 — Do Your Job, Document, and Communicate

1
Do your job — fulfill your defined responsibilities only; do not compensate for systemic gaps.
2
Maintain professionalism — keep all communications measured and factual, regardless of the dysfunction around you.
3
Document daily — log tasks completed, requests received, help sought, and problems encountered.
4
Ask clarifying questions in writing — end emails about unclear or unfair requests with a direct question to prompt a response and create a record.
How this applies to you When leadership creates a broken situation, your job is to document what you did, what you requested, and what was unclear — not to fix the whole system. Turning concerns into written questions shifts accountability back where it belongs and protects your record.

Framework 2 — Strategic Use of FMLA / Short-Term Disability

1
Recognize the pattern — notice when the workplace is consistently harmful and unresponsive.
2
Know your rights — FMLA and short-term disability apply to mental health and burnout, not just physical emergencies.
3
Plan intentionally — use any leave for genuine rest, reflection, and concrete next steps such as a job search.
How this applies to you You have short-term disability coverage available for up to 12 weeks. Taking leave is not surrender — it is a deliberate intervention to stop the damage, restore your functioning, and create space to plan your next move from a position of stability.

Practical Instructions

  • Restrict your work to tasks within your defined role — do not step in to fix others' failures.
  • Keep a daily log of tasks, requests, communications, and any issues that arise.
  • When expectations are unclear or a request exceeds your role, respond with a professional email that ends in a clarifying question.
  • Return to at least one restorative activity (e.g., jiu jitsu, adequate sleep) as a non-negotiable this week.

Assignment

Starting today, keep a brief end-of-day work log: what you did, what was requested of you, any issues encountered, and whether anything fell outside your role. For any problematic request, draft a professional email summarizing the situation and closing with a clarifying question. Save everything in one secure location and bring examples to your next session for review.

Key Takeaways

Chronic workplace dysfunction is not your fault, even when you're left to manage the fallout.

Over-functioning to cover others' gaps leads directly to burnout and shame.

Documentation is your most practical defense against misplaced blame.

Clarifying questions in writing create accountability and a factual record.

FMLA and short-term disability are legitimate mental health tools, not last resorts.

Self-blame is a learned response — it can be challenged with evidence and consistent practice.

Bottom Line

The pattern driving your distress — absorbing blame for problems you did not create — is identifiable, understandable, and changeable. Focusing strictly on your defined role, documenting your work, and communicating professionally are not passive strategies; they actively shift responsibility back to the organization and protect your standing.

Whether you stay or eventually leave, your health comes first. Rest is not a reward for finishing everything — it is a requirement for functioning well. The tools from this session are designed to reduce harm now and create the clarity needed to make your next move on your own terms.
