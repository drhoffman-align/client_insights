Finding Stillness

Creating Space for Yourself Amidst Chaos

The Situation

Life at home is relentless — ongoing renovations, caring for a young child, a partner dealing with illness, and frequent travel have created a state of chronic overwhelm. Physical symptoms including shoulder and back pain, disrupted sleep, and persistent fatigue reflect how little genuine recovery time exists.

Even during moments that are technically "alone," available time gets absorbed by errands, appointments, and household management. Attempts at self-care — massages, solo trips, retreats — offer brief relief but demand their own planning and energy, and don't produce the deep rest that's missing.

Core Problem

The central issue is not a shortage of alone time, but a habitual pattern of filling every available moment with activity. Whether it's tasks, chores, or even planned self-care, nothing in the current routine creates true mental space for rest or reflection.

This "always in motion" pattern has become automatic — even when there is no external demand, the pull toward productivity remains. Without interrupting this cycle, the clarity and peace that feel so distant will stay out of reach.

Understanding the Dynamics

Restorative StillnessTime spent without any agenda, task, or obligation — simply being present. It is unfamiliar and may feel unproductive, but it is the foundation of mental recovery.
Self-Care vs. StillnessSelf-care activities (massages, trips) are beneficial but require effort and planning. True stillness asks for nothing — it is the absence of obligation, not a different kind of task.
Decision FatigueConstantly being the default decision-maker depletes mental resources. Protected stillness creates the space needed to process and plan, reducing reactive and regretted choices.
Action OrientationA deeply ingrained pattern of staying in motion — often adaptive earlier in life — that now works against rest and reflection. It can be recognized and gradually reshaped.

Framework: Intentional Stillness and Protected Time

1
Identify a consistent daily window — e.g., 8:30–10:00 a.m. — to be alone and undisturbed.
2
Block it on your calendar as a recurring, non-negotiable appointment.
3
Eliminate all tasks, errands, chores, and effort-requiring self-care from this window.
4
Use the time to sit, rest, or simply be — no agenda, no output required.
5
Once stillness feels more familiar, use part of the time to process decisions or set intentions.
6
If daily feels too ambitious, begin with 2–3 days per week and build from there.
How This Applies Currently, solo time defaults to productivity. The shift is concrete: instead of running an errand or coordinating with a housekeeper, you sit quietly — indoors, outside, wherever feels calm. No planning required. This is the difference between being "waited on" and doing more self-care. The calendar block signals to yourself and others that this time is protected, not available.

Practical Instructions

  • Choose a specific daily window and add it to your calendar as a recurring block.
  • Let family and household know you are unavailable during that time — keep it brief and firm.
  • If thoughts or to-do's surface during the window, set them aside — they can wait.
  • Start with 2–3 days per week if daily feels like too much; consistency matters more than duration.

Assignment

Before the next session, review your weekly schedule and identify where a daily stillness window could realistically fit. Choose a location — at home or elsewhere — where you can be undisturbed. Block the time on your calendar and protect it at least once this week. Notice any resistance, discomfort, or relief, and bring those observations to the next session.

Key Takeaways

The problem is not too little alone time — it's too little protected, obligation-free time.
Filling time with tasks, even self-care, prevents genuine mental rest.
Scheduled stillness is a clinical tool, not a luxury or indulgence.
Decision fatigue is real — mental space is required to plan and choose well.
The habit of constant activity can be recognized and deliberately interrupted.
Small, consistent windows of stillness produce meaningful change over time.

Bottom Line

This session drew a clear distinction between being alone and truly giving yourself space to rest and process. Exhaustion and overwhelm are valid — and they are being sustained by a pattern of relentless activity that leaves no room for recovery. The goal is not to add another item to your list, but to protect time that already exists from being consumed by demands.

The next step is simple and specific: identify the window, put it on your calendar, and protect it. Over time, this practice supports clearer thinking, better decisions, and a return to the sense of peace that feels distant right now. Rest is not a reward for finishing everything — it is part of what makes everything else sustainable.
