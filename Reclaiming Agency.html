🦄 Reclaiming Agency: Building Identity and Strategic Self-Advocacy in Career and Life

A written answer to "who am I" is easier to stand on than one built under pressure

The Situation

A media-related freelance role is being weighed against a search for something more stable and fulfilling, with an upcoming interview at the Smithsonian's American Women's History Museum bringing real questions about how to present authentically. People-pleasing, self-doubt about intelligence and value, and a habit of downplaying achievements show up repeatedly.

ADHD adds its own layer, affecting focus and motivation, alongside an adjustment to a new, earlier wake-up routine. Genuine excitement about new opportunities sits right next to anxiety about performance and acceptance.

Core Problem

A pattern of seeking external validation, especially in professional settings, while minimizing personal strengths and needs, shows up as people-pleasing, self-sabotage — not giving full effort in order to avoid raising expectations — and difficulty asserting value in interviews.

There's a mismatch between actual capability and the internal narrative running underneath it, producing underconfidence and a real gap in self-advocacy.

"Knowing who you are will come up for the rest of your life. Seeing your identity on paper will help you sort of solidify who you are or the foundations of who you are."

Understanding the Dynamics

People-Pleasing

Prioritizing others' needs and approval over your own, often at real personal cost — visible here in both work and interview settings.

ADHD

A neurodevelopmental condition affecting attention and focus, with scattered thinking off medication and genuine hyperfocus when motivation is high.

External Validation

Relying on others' approval to feel good about yourself — here, self-worth fluctuates with feedback from editors and interviewers.

Self-Sabotage

Deliberately holding back effort to avoid setting expectations that might later disappoint — protective in intent, costly in practice.

Reflective Processing

Working through insights outside of session — without it, therapy's impact stays contained to the hour it happens in.

Framework A

Identity Clarification

1
List core characteristics that define who you are.
2
Keep the list accessible — on your phone — and update it as insights come up.
3
Use the list as a foundation when your identity is questioned or challenged.
4
Refer to it before interviews or stressful situations to ground yourself.
Applied Intelligence and value get doubted, especially after negative feedback. Listing and affirming actual strengths ahead of time counteracts that doubt before it has room to take over.

Framework B

Strategic Interview Preparation

1
Review the job posting and compare it against your resume.
2
Identify unique skills and experiences that set you apart.
3
Prepare concrete examples that demonstrate your impact and expertise.
4
Anticipate what the interviewer is actually looking for — skills, fit, long-term potential.
5
Practice communicating both technical ability and personal qualities.
Applied Job requirements already get reviewed and matched to experience, but unique contributions tend to get downplayed. The framework pushes toward highlighting metrics-driven successes and direct museum familiarity, not just qualifications on paper.

Framework C

Self-Advocacy and Power Retention

1
Recognize when you're giving away your power — over-accommodating, minimizing yourself.
2
Shift focus from pleasing others to meeting your own needs and goals.
3
Use your identity list to reinforce your value.
4
Practice asserting needs in low-stakes situations, building toward higher-stakes ones.
Applied A people-pleasing tendency leads to overperforming or underperforming based on others' expectations rather than actual goals. Recognizing that pattern is the first step toward advocating for real needs, in interviews and in daily life.

Giving Away Power vs. Retaining It

Giving it away

  • Over-accommodating others
  • Minimizing real achievements
  • Worth tied to the latest feedback

Retaining it

  • Asserting your own needs directly
  • Naming strengths without qualifiers
  • Worth grounded in your own list
"A lot of the ways you show up in the world is giving up a lot of your power to other people who don't care."

Practical Instructions

  • Before answering "tell me about yourself" in an interview, practice saying one strength out loud without immediately following it with a qualifier or apology.
  • When you catch yourself preparing to underperform to avoid raising expectations, name that instinct out loud before deciding whether to act on it.
  • After receiving critical feedback, wait until the next day to decide how much weight it actually deserves.
  • Pick one small daily interaction where you'd normally defer, and practice stating your actual preference instead.
  • At the end of each week, note one moment you advocated for yourself, no matter how minor it felt in the moment.

Assignment

Write down at least 5 qualities that define you, save the list on your phone, and update it as new insights come up. Refer to it before interviews or whenever criticism triggers self-doubt. Come ready to discuss your list and any situations where you used it.

Key Takeaways

A documented, accessible list of your own characteristics is a tool you can return to under pressure, not just a one-time exercise.
Downplaying unique strengths in an interview works against the actual goal of being seen accurately.
Deliberately underperforming to avoid raising expectations is still a form of self-sabotage, even when it feels protective.
Hyperfocus shows motivation is available — the work is directing it toward your own goals, not just external demands.
A consistent wake time paired with reflection on the bigger picture builds self-awareness that a rushed morning doesn't.
Self-worth that moves with each piece of feedback is worth anchoring to something steadier.
Processing insights outside of session is part of what makes therapy's impact last between appointments.
Practicing self-advocacy in low-stakes moments builds the capacity needed for higher-stakes ones.

Bottom Line

The pattern of seeking approval while minimizing real strengths isn't a confidence problem in the usual sense. It's a habit of outsourcing self-worth to whoever's giving feedback at the moment, which means it fluctuates by design.

Having a written, specific answer to "who am I" available before it's needed is what makes it possible to hold steady when someone questions your value, rather than scrambling to reconstruct an answer under pressure.

Self-advocacy isn't selfishness, and it isn't constant self-promotion. It's the simple redirection of energy that's been going toward managing other people's expectations back toward meeting your own.
