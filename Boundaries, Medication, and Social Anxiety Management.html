Alignment Psychiatry

Navigating Disconnection, Boundaries, and Medication

Building Clarity and Self-Compassion

The Situation

Persistent low mood and motivation are ongoing concerns, with stimulant medication providing focus but sometimes worsening depressive symptoms when not actively engaged. A new medication (fluoxetine) is being introduced to provide greater mood stability.

Socially, there is a pattern of feeling isolated and disconnected — both historically and in current friendships. A close friendship has become strained after the friend reconciled with an ex, leaving significant emotional ambiguity. Anxiety about being perceived or judged is making social situations and social media feel difficult to navigate.

Core Problem

The central pattern is overextending in relationships — giving more support than is returned — which leads to emotional exhaustion, resentment, and difficulty knowing when or how to set limits. This tendency to over-function is rooted in feeling responsible for others' wellbeing.

Compounding this is a gap between internal experience and external reality: the feeling of being watched, judged, or rejected is real, but it often does not reflect what others are actually thinking or doing. These distorted beliefs drive avoidance, which deepens isolation and reinforces the cycle.

Understanding the Dynamics

Cognitive DistortionThe belief that others are constantly watching or judging you — when in reality, most people are focused on themselves.
Emotional BluntingA possible SSRI side effect where both high and low emotions feel muted — the goal is a floor, not a ceiling, for feelings.
Over-ResponsibilityTaking on the emotional burdens of others beyond what the relationship warrants, often at the cost of one's own wellbeing.
Black Box WarningAn FDA alert that fluoxetine can increase suicidal thoughts in young adults, especially early in treatment — requires close monitoring.

Frameworks

1 — Addressing the Elephant in the Room

  • 1Identify the unspoken issue causing ongoing tension.
  • 2State the facts of the situation clearly, without blame.
  • 3Express your feelings and what you need going forward.
  • 4Allow the other person to respond and accept their choice.
  • 5Decide how to proceed based on clarity, not obligation.
Applied here: After the friend's birthday, send a direct message stating what you've noticed, how it has affected you, and what you hope for — without waiting for her to bring it up first.

2 — Gradual Exposure to Being Perceived

  • 1Engage in a low-stakes setting where you feel observed (gym, minor social post).
  • 2Notice the anxious belief — "everyone is watching me."
  • 3Reframe it: most people are absorbed in their own experience.
  • 4Tolerate the discomfort without avoiding, then gradually increase exposure.
Applied here: Post a small, authentic update or simply show up to the gym — each instance of staying present rather than withdrawing builds resilience over time.

Practical Instructions

  • For the friendship: wait until after her birthday, draft a clear message expressing what you've noticed and what you need, then send it without attaching to a specific outcome.
  • For social media anxiety: start with low-stakes engagement, remind yourself that most people are not scrutinizing your posts.
  • For boundaries: decide in advance what you are willing to give (e.g., a card and flowers), communicate it clearly, and don't add more out of guilt.
  • For medication: take fluoxetine 20mg daily as prescribed, monitor for side effects, and contact your provider immediately if mood worsens or suicidal thoughts emerge.

Assignment

After your friend's birthday, draft and send a message that names what you've observed in the relationship, how it has affected you, and what you hope for — whether that's closure or reconnection. Send it without expectation of a specific reply, then reflect on how it feels to have acted from clarity rather than waiting in ambiguity. Bring your emotional response and any updates to the next session.

Key Takeaways

1.Low mood and motivation are real and may be intensified by stimulant medication.
2.Over-giving without reciprocity leads to exhaustion — not to closer relationships.
3.Addressing conflict directly uses less energy than prolonged ambiguity.
4.Most people are not scrutinizing you — the belief that they are is a distortion.
5.Being a good person does not require self-sacrifice or tolerating mistreatment.
6.Fluoxetine takes 4–6 weeks to work; monitor side effects and report concerns promptly.

Bottom Line

The core work right now is learning to stay present — in relationships, in social spaces, and in your own emotional experience — rather than withdrawing to avoid discomfort. Avoidance protects in the short term but compounds isolation and anxiety over time.

Setting limits, speaking directly, and tolerating being seen are not signs of selfishness. They are the practical skills through which self-respect and genuine connection are built. Medication can support mood stability, but the relational and behavioral work is what creates lasting change.
